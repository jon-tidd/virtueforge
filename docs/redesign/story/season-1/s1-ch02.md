## Chapter-book telling

Last time: a cold, wet fox tapped at the last lit window in Candlemere, with news that the beacon on Wardlow had gone out.

In the morning, Candlemere was grey. No chimney smoked, not one lamp flickered, and every window on the lane looked as sleepy as a yawn. Somebody had to carry Grandma's flame to Wardlow, and soon.

"Let's ask the neighbors," said Hugh. "A grown-up will go."

Grandma stood in the doorway, where she could see the whole lane. "Every door," she said. "And stay where I can see you."

So off they went down the lane. Hugh went in front, and Alfie counted the doors. Clara held the very tip of Ember's tail, which Ember allowed, because it was Tuesday.

The baker opened the bakehouse door in a puff of flour. "Someone should," he said. "But not I. The bread won't bake itself."

"Good morning," said Ember, very politely.

But all the baker heard was *yip, yip*. "Noisy fox," he said, not unkindly, and shut the door.

Ember sniffed. Nobody had ever called her noisy before.

Next door, the miller came out slowly, rubbing his knees. "Not I," he said. "These old knees won't climb Wardlow."

Then Reeve Osric hurried past with his arms full of tally-sticks. "Yes, yes," he said. "We'll see to it tomorrow."

"How many tomorrows has that been?" whispered Alfie.

"One, two, three, four, lots," whispered Ember.

And so it went, all along the lane. Everybody agreed that someone should go. But nobody was willing to be the one. At every door the answer was the same: "Not I."

Then Will, the baker's boy, came pelting round the corner. "I'll go right now," he shouted, "and I dare anybody to stop me!" Off he ran into the old holloway, with no light at all.

The holloway was a sunken lane under the trees, dark even at noon.

Hugh wanted to dare the dark too, and run in after him. Alfie wanted to run home and hide under the quilt. Nobody moved.

*Splosh!* Before anyone could count to twenty, Will came back out of the dark, muddy from his boots to his ears, and a little sheepish.

"There's a puddle," said Will, "in the dark."

Nobody laughed. Clara went and stood right next to the muddy boy, so Will wouldn't be muddy all on his own.

By the Moot Hall door sat old Hild the wick-woman, with her tray of needles, thread and lamp-wicks. Her donkey, Dun, dozed at her side. On the door behind her hung an old notice.

Hugh read it out loud: "Beacon Night. All welcome. Bring one stick."

Old Brock lay on the step, the way the old dog always did, and did not get up for anyone. Clara stopped to pat him anyway.

"Good morning," said Ember.

"Good morning, fox," said Hild, as if foxes wished her good morning every day of the week.

Hugh and Alfie stared. The wick-woman could understand Ember too!

Then Hild beckoned Hugh close, while Ember tried to count the wicks and got as far as lots.

She looked at Hugh for a long, quiet moment. "How big must a person be to carry a lantern?" she asked.

Hugh opened his mouth, and shut it again. Hild only waited, the way old folk wait for a kettle to sing.

[[Pause & ask]]

Then Hild gave each of them something from her tray that wasn't on it at all: a question, to keep in a pocket. "For when I'm not there," she said.

To Hugh: "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?" To Alfie: "What else could we try?" And to Clara: "Who needs help?"

Last of all, Hild put a little brass bell into Clara's hand. "Ring it if you ever lose them," she said. "Stand still, ring, and call."

Then Hild went back to her wicks, as if nothing much had happened at all.

"Who needs help?" whispered Clara, looking at Will, muddy and bedraggled on the step. Then out went the bell, and a hand too. "You carry the bell."

Will took the bell, and the hand. *Ting-a-ling!* Not one bit sheepish now, Will rang it all the way back up the lane, very loudly and very proudly. It was the only bright sound in the whole grey lane, and here and there a curtain twitched.

Ember trotted beside him with her ears folded flat, looking pained.

At the cottage gate, Will gave the bell back with a grin. "When the beacon's lit again," he said, "I'll carry a stick up Wardlow myself."

Back by the fire, Grandma listened to all of it.

Hugh looked into the fire, thinking about the long road and the dark, and his tummy went wobbly. Nobody on the whole lane had been willing. Then Hugh stood up, held an invisible lantern up high, and took one step forward.

"I'll help," he said. "With a proper light, and all of us together. Lantern up!"

A hush fell over the cottage. Then the fire gave a small pop, as if it agreed.

"And me," said Alfie, "if there are apples." In went the apples, one at a time.

"One, two, three, four, lots," said Ember helpfully.

"Seven," said Alfie.

"And I shall come too," said Ember. "Somebody has to know the way, and foxes are extremely good at roads."

Clara pulled on her boots, wrong feet first, and swapped them round with no help at all. "Me too!" said Clara.

Grandma laughed. "Nobody's too small to carry a light," she said. "But nobody carries it alone."

That night the little brass bell sat on the mantel, next to the note from home. Alfie used the backpack for a pillow. Clara fell asleep with one boot still on, and Ember curled round her like a warm red scarf. Hugh fell asleep last. Hild's question was still there, the way a smooth stone stays in your pocket.

## Picture-book telling

Candlemere was grey, and somebody had to carry Grandma's flame to Wardlow. "Let's ask the neighbors," said Hugh. Grandma watched from the doorway.

The baker was too busy, and the miller's old knees were too stiff. Reeve Osric would see to it tomorrow. At every door, the answer was the same: "Not I."

Then Will, the baker's boy, ran into the dark holloway with no light! Hugh wanted to dare the dark too, and run in after him. Alfie wanted to run home and hide under the quilt. Nobody moved.

*Splosh!* Back came Will, muddy to the ears and a little sheepish. "There's a puddle," said Will, "in the dark." No one laughed, and Clara stood right next to the muddy boy.

Old Hild sat by the Moot Hall door, with Dun the donkey. Old Brock the dog lay on the step. Hild looked at Hugh for a long, quiet moment. "How big must a person be to carry a lantern?" asked Hild.

Next, Hild handed out pocket questions. To Hugh: "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?" To Alfie: "What else could we try?" And to Clara: "Who needs help?"

Then Hild gave Clara a little brass bell. "Ring it if you ever lose them. Stand still, ring, and call."

"Who needs help?" whispered Clara. Will did! Out went the bell, and a hand to hold. "You carry the bell."

*Ting-a-ling!* Not one bit sheepish now, Will rang the bell all the way home. Ember's ears went flat, and here and there a curtain twitched.

By the fire, Hugh felt wobbly inside, and held up an invisible lantern. "I'll help," he said. "With a proper light, and all of us together. Lantern up!"

"And me," said Alfie, "if there are apples." In went the apples, one at a time. And Ember was coming too, because foxes are extremely good at roads.

That night the brass bell sat on the mantel, by the note from home. Everyone fell asleep by the low fire, ready for the long road. Ember curled round Clara like a warm red scarf.
