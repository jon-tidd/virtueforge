## Chapter-book telling

Last time: a cold, wet fox tapped at the last lit window in Candlemere, with news that the beacon on Wardlow had gone out.

In the morning, Candlemere was grey. No chimney smoked, not one lamp flickered, and every window on the lane looked as sleepy as a yawn. Somebody had to carry Grandma's flame to Wardlow, and soon.

"Let's ask the neighbors," said Hugh. "A grown-up will go."

Grandma stood in the doorway, where she could see the whole lane. "Every door," she said. "And stay where I can see you."

So off they went down the lane. Hugh went in front, and Alfie counted the doors. Clara walked beside Ember, who held her magnificent tail high out of the puddles.

The baker opened the bakehouse door in a puff of flour. "Someone should," he said. "But not I. The bread won't bake itself."

"Good morning," said Ember, very politely.

But all the baker heard was *yip, yip*. "Noisy fox," he said, not unkindly, and shut the door.

Ember sniffed. Nobody had ever called her noisy before.

Next door, the miller came out slowly, rubbing his knees. "Not I," he said. "These old knees won't climb Wardlow."

Then Reeve Osric hurried past with his arms full of tally-sticks. "Yes, yes," he said. "We'll see to it tomorrow."

At one window a face peeped out. "Too far, in the dark," it whispered, and the curtain dropped.

"How many dark windows will there be by tomorrow?" whispered Alfie.

"One, two, three, four, lots," whispered Ember.

Everybody agreed that someone should go. But nobody was willing to be the one. At every door the answer was the same: "Not I."

Then Will, the baker's boy, came pelting round the corner. "I'll go right now," he shouted, "and I dare anybody to stop me!" Off he ran into the old holloway, with no light at all.

The holloway, a sunken track under the trees, was dark even at noon, though its mouth was in sight of Grandma's doorway.

Hugh wanted to take the dare too, and run in after him. Alfie wanted to run home and hide under the quilt. Nobody moved.

*Splosh!* Before anyone could count to twenty, Will was back, muddy from top to toe, a little sheepish, and looking hard at his boots.

"There's a puddle," said Will, "in the dark."

Nobody laughed. Clara went and stood right next to the muddy boy, so Will wouldn't be muddy all on his own.

By the Moot Hall door, in plain sight of Grandma's doorway, sat old Hild the wick-woman with her tray of wicks. Grandma had bought wicks from her for years. Hild's donkey, Dun, dozed at her side. On the door behind her hung an old notice.

Hugh read it out loud: "Beacon Night. All welcome. Bring one stick, and a friend."

Old Brock lay on the step, and did not get up for anyone. Clara stopped to pat him anyway.

"Good morning," said Ember.

"Good morning, fox," said Hild, as if foxes said good morning to her every day.

Hugh and Alfie stared. The wick-woman could understand Ember too!

Then Hild beckoned Hugh close, while Ember tried to count the wicks and got as far as lots.

Hild looked at Hugh for a long, quiet moment. "How big must a person be to carry a lantern?" she asked.

Hugh opened his mouth, and shut it again. Hild only waited, the way old folk wait for a kettle to sing.

[[Pause & ask]]

Then Hild gave each of them something from her tray that wasn't on it at all: a question, to keep in a pocket. "For when I'm not there," said Hild.

To Hugh: "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?" To Alfie: "What else could we try?" And to Clara: "Who needs help?"

Last of all, Hild put a little brass bell into Clara's hand. "Ring it if you ever lose them," said Hild. "Stand still, ring, and call."

"Who needs help?" whispered Clara, looking at Will, muddy and bedraggled on the step. Then out went the bell, and a hand too. "You carry the bell."

Will took the bell, and the hand. *Ting-a-ling!* Not one bit sheepish now, Will rang it all the way back up the lane, loud and proud. Here and there a curtain twitched.

Ember trotted beside him with her ears folded flat, looking pained.

At the cottage gate, Will gave the bell back with a grin. "On Beacon Night," he said, "I'll carry a stick up Wardlow myself."

"Stand still, ring, and call," said Clara, holding the bell up high.

Back by the fire, Grandma listened to all of it.

Hugh looked into the fire, thinking about the long road and the dark, and his tummy went wobbly. Nobody on the whole lane had been willing. Then Hugh stood up, held an invisible lantern up high, and took one step forward.

"I'll help," he said. "With a proper light, and all of us together. Lantern up!"

A hush fell over the cottage. Then the fire gave a small pop, as if it agreed.

"And me," said Alfie. In went the apples, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven.

"One, two, three, four, lots," said Ember helpfully.

"Seven!" said Alfie.

"And I shall come too," said Ember. "Somebody has to know the way, and foxes are extremely good at roads."

Clara pulled on her boots, wrong feet first, and swapped them round with no help at all. "Me too!" said Clara.

Grandma smiled at them all. "Nobody's too small to carry a light," she said. "But nobody carries it alone."

That night the brass bell sat on the mantel by the note from home. Alfie used the backpack for a pillow. Clara fell asleep with one boot still on, and Ember curled round her like a warm red scarf. Hugh fell asleep last, with Hild's question still there, like a smooth stone in a pocket.

## Picture-book telling

Candlemere was grey, and somebody had to carry Grandma's flame to Wardlow. "Let's ask the neighbors," said Hugh. Grandma watched from the doorway.

The baker was too busy, and the miller's old knees were too stiff. Reeve Osric would see to it tomorrow. At every door, the answer was the same: "Not I."

Then Will, the baker's boy, ran into the dark holloway with no light! Hugh wanted to run after him, and Alfie wanted to run home. Nobody moved.

*Splosh!* went the holloway, and back came Will, muddy and a little sheepish. "There's a puddle," said Will, "in the dark." No one laughed, and Clara stood right next to the muddy boy.

Old Hild sat by the Moot Hall door, with Dun and Old Brock. Hild looked at Hugh for a long, quiet moment. "How big must a person be to carry a lantern?" asked Hild.

Next, Hild handed out pocket questions. To Hugh: "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?" To Alfie: "What else could we try?" And to Clara: "Who needs help?"

Then Hild gave Clara a little brass bell. "Ring it if you ever lose them. Stand still, ring, and call."

"Who needs help?" whispered Clara. Will did! So out went the bell and a hand: "You carry the bell."

*Ting-a-ling!* Not one bit sheepish now, Will rang the bell all the way home. At the gate, Clara held up the bell and said, "Stand still, ring, and call."

By the fire, Hugh felt wobbly inside, and held up an invisible lantern. "I'll help," he said. "With a proper light, and all of us together. Lantern up!"

In went the apples, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven. "Me too!" said Clara, and pulled on her own boots.

"Nobody's too small to carry a light," said Grandma. That night everyone fell asleep by the low fire. Ember curled round Clara like a warm red scarf.
