## Chapter-book telling

Last time: at every door on the lane the answer was "Not I," until Hugh said, "I'll help."

Today the flame would leave Candlemere. After lunch, Grandma took down the old brass lantern. "The Keepers' Lantern," she said.

Grandma held the lantern close to the hearth, its little door shut. "A light that's taken will not catch," she said. "A light that's given will. So somebody has to ask."

"Please," whispered Clara.

Everyone waited in a hush, the way you wait for a sneeze…and the flame hopped across. In it went, through the chimney, and the whole lantern glowed like a jar of honey.

"I wasn't worried at all," said Ember, from behind the log basket. Her ears went flat. *Atchoo!*

"Now, the lantern breath," said Grandma. "In slowly through your nose. Out so gently that a flame would only lean."

Everyone tried it, even Ember, who was always willing. Nothing happened at all, and she looked terribly pleased.

Then Grandma set the lantern on the table and ran a finger round its base, where names were scratched. "That one's mine," she said. "I carried this lantern to Wardlow when I was seven."

Hugh read the names, sounding out the hard ones. One was worn so smooth that nobody could read it.

"Whose is that one, worn smooth?" asked Alfie.

Grandma ran a thumb over the smooth place. "That's not mine to tell."

Ember looked at the smooth name. She said nothing at all.

"You have my yes," said Grandma. "By the old law, any child who carries this lantern is fed and housed at every door on the Keepers' Way." Then Grandma cast on a red scarf: "One row for every night you're away."

At the gate there were hugs all round, and a proper goodbye. Then off they went in the gold of the afternoon, Hugh carrying the lantern by its ring handle, as steady as steady.

All afternoon the Keepers' Way ran west between tall hedges, past stubble fields and a mill-pond and a line of old grey milestones. Ember trotted in front with her tail up like a flag, and stepped round every single puddle. "Wet paws," she said, "are the enemy of a good fox."

The hedges were full of rosehips, as red as Ember's coat. A blackbird followed them for a whole field, as if it wanted to see where the lantern was going.

Once, a brown hare peeked out of the hedge, stared at the lantern, and lolloped off as if it had just remembered a kettle on the boil.

Ember bowed to it most politely, but it did not bow back. "No manners at all," she sniffed.

Hugh read every milestone out loud, sounding out the old carved letters: how far to the next village, and how far back home. Home was already quite a long way.

Clara hummed a hearth song from home to every cow they passed, and every cow looked up to listen. Alfie counted the cows: "One, two, three, four, five, six!"

"One, two, three, four, lots," said Ember, helping.

And in the bottom of the backpack, under the apples, rode one small treasure from home: a smooth grey pebble from the cottage path, for luck.

By the time the sun was low and orange, Candlemere was only a smudge of roofs behind them. Nobody talked about it, but everyone looked back once.

At sunset the road passed a hollow oak as wide as a doorway. Further on, by a milestone, stood a shepherd's hut. Then the wind rose, gust by gust.

*Whoo*, went the wind, and inside the lantern the flame began to flicker. It shrank and shrank to a tiny blue bead.

Hugh's heart thumped. Part of him wanted to dash on into the dark. And part of him wanted to run home.

Hild's pocket question was still there, like a smooth stone. Hugh asked it out loud: "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?"

[[Pause & ask]]

Another gust came, harder. "Excuse me," said Ember to the wind, very grandly. "That is my tail, and I should like it back."

"Ember, what should we do?" asked Hugh.

"I know the way home," said Ember gently. "The way on is yours."

"The silly thing is rushing on in the dark with a flame this small," said Hugh slowly. "The brave thing is stopping, and getting it out of the wind."

Hugh held the lantern steady, took a lantern breath, the way Grandma had shown them, and looked around.

"Big stone!" said Clara, pointing.

It was an old grey milestone, with a little lantern carved on top. Hugh read the carving: "West to Wardlow, a long way. East to home. Keep the light."

The wind roared past one side of the stone, but the other side was still. "The lee," said Hugh, and carried the lantern round into it: Grandma's word for the side the wind can't reach.

Ember sat down on the still side. "Much better," she said. "My tail agrees."

Alfie looked at the dark, and his tummy went wobbly. Then Alfie held an invisible lantern up high and stepped round into the wind. "I'll help, and I'll be the wall. Lantern up!"

Hugh wanted to keep holding the lantern, just to be sure. But Grandma's rule was hands on knees, never on a lantern once it was set down.

Clara gave a yawn as big as a barn door, so Ember wrapped that magnificent red tail round her like a scarf. "Night night, little flame," whispered Clara.

Inside the lantern the blue bead flickered and bowed low, like someone very tired. It held on, just.

Far behind them, Grandma would be knitting the first row of the red scarf, by a fire that stayed awake. Hugh held on to that.

Hugh set the lantern down in the lee of the milestone, and he and Alfie made a wall with their backs to the wind. Clara was already asleep in Ember's tail.

Ember lifted her nose. "Someone else walked this road tonight," she said. "Someone I know."

"Who?" whispered Alfie.

"Sleep now." The flame was only a blue bead. Would it hold till morning?

## Picture-book telling

Today the flame would leave Candlemere, in the old brass Keepers' Lantern. Grandma held it close to the fire, its little door shut. "Somebody has to ask."

"Please," whispered Clara. Everyone waited in a hush…and the flame hopped across. The whole lantern glowed like a jar of honey.

"Now, the lantern breath," said Grandma. "In slowly through your nose. Out so gently that a flame would only lean."

Names were scratched round the lantern's brass base, and one was worn smooth. "That's not mine to tell," said Grandma. Ember looked at it for a long time, and said nothing.

At the gate there were hugs, and a proper goodbye. Grandma waved and waved. Off went everyone, with Hugh carrying the lantern as steady as steady.

All afternoon the road ran west, and Ember stepped round every puddle. Soon Candlemere was only a smudge of roofs. Nobody said so, but everyone looked back once.

At sunset everyone passed a hollow oak, as wide as a doorway. Further on stood a shepherd's hut. Then *whoo*, the wind rose, and the flame shrank to a tiny blue bead.

In the dark, Hugh stopped, and asked the pocket question. "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?" The answer came: "Rushing on is silly. Stopping is brave."

Ember wouldn't choose the way on, so Hugh took a lantern breath. "Big stone!" said Clara, pointing. It was an old milestone, with one side still, out of the wind.

"I'll help," said Alfie, holding an invisible lantern up high. "I'll be the wall. Lantern up!" The blue flame flickered and bowed, like someone very tired, but it held on.

Hugh set the lantern down in the lee. Alfie helped make a wall, hands on knees. "Someone I know walked this road," said Ember, with a happy swish.

Far away, Grandma knitted by a fire that stayed awake. Clara fell asleep in Ember's tail. The flame was only a blue bead. Would it hold till morning?
