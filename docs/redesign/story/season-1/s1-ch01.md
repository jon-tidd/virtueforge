## Chapter-book telling

The lights of Candlemere were going out, one by one, and nobody knew why. Tonight Clara watched from the window as the lamp over the Moot Hall door shrank to the size of a seed, and went out. Now the only light left in the whole village was Grandma Ruth's fire, crackling behind her. Then something tapped on the glass. *Tap. Tap. Tap.*

Clara pulled back the curtain. On the sill sat a red fox, wet to the ears, with a leaf stuck to one of them. "Good evening," said the fox. "I'm so sorry to bother you."

Hugh came to stand beside her. Alfie pushed his glasses up his nose. "Foxes don't talk," he said.

"This one does," said the fox. "And she is very cold."

Clara looked back. "Grandma, she's cold!"

Grandma was already smiling at the fox, and she nodded.

"Come in, fox," said Clara.

Hugh lifted the latch, and in she came, and shook herself dry all over the hearth-rug, and all over Alfie.

Grandma looked at the fox for a long moment, the way you look at an old friend. "Well," she said. "You've got your grandmother's tail."

"Everyone says that," said the fox. "My name is Ember. And the beacon on Wardlow has gone out."

Clara looked at Hugh. Hugh looked at Alfie. And that is how it began.

For a moment nobody said a word, and a hush fell over the cottage. The fox dripped on the rug. So did Alfie.

Ember turned round three times in front of the fire and spread out her tail to dry. It was a very bedraggled tail, all drips and droops. "Do mind my magnificent tail," she said. Soon she began to steam gently, like a pudding.

"You're steaming," said Alfie.

"I am not steaming," said Ember. "Foxes don't steam. Foxes glow."

Then Ember's tummy went *rrrumble*. She looked up at the ceiling, as if the sound had come from somewhere else entirely.

"Food first," said Grandma. "Questions after. What does a cold fox like to eat?"

"Sausages," said Ember at once. "Or cheese rinds. I'm not fussy." She thought about it. "Sausages."

"We haven't got any sausages," said Alfie.

"Then I shall be very brave about it," said Ember.

There was a cheese rind on the pantry shelf. But the pantry candle had flickered low that afternoon, like all the others, and gone *pfft*. Now the pantry was as dark as the inside of a boot.

Alfie shuffled a little closer to the fire. Hugh was already jumping up to dash straight in without looking. But Clara spoke first: "I'll help!"

Clara's tummy gave a wobble, but Candlemere had an old saying for dim places. So Clara held an invisible lantern up high and whispered it: "Lantern up!"

Grandma waited in the pantry doorway, the way grown-ups wait when they know you can do it. Back came Clara with the rind in both hands.

The bedraggled fox ate it in one gulp. Then she licked her whiskers and bowed, rather grandly. "Thank you," she said. "That was the finest cheese rind in the whole Westering."

"I couldn't possibly have another," said Ember. Then she gazed at the pantry door for a long, hopeful while.

[[Pause & ask]]

"How can a faraway fire make our lamps go out?" asked Alfie.

Ember sat up very straight. "All the fires in the Westering are one family," she said, "and the beacon on Wardlow is their grandmother. When she went out, they all grew tired and sad. And one by one, they began to fall asleep."

"Poor fires," whispered Clara, and laid a hand on the fox's warm side.

Grandma shook her head. "Not this one," she said. "It has never once gone out."

Down below, the mere lay black and still. Once it had held every lit window in Candlemere upside down. Tonight it held only one: Grandma's window.

Alfie counted the dark windows, one finger for each: the mill, the bakehouse, the Moot Hall. Soon there were no fingers left.

"One, two, three, four, lots," said Ember sadly.

"Lots isn't a number," said Alfie.

"It is a very large number," said Ember, "and it gets larger every night."

Then Hugh noticed something: Grandma had understood every word the fox said.

"You can understand Ember too," said Hugh.

Grandma only smiled. "I've known a fox or two," she said, and put another log on the fire.

"What happens if nobody lights the beacon again?" asked Hugh.

Grandma looked into the fire for a long while. "Then one night, this fire will grow tired too," she said. "But not tonight."

On the mantel stood the note Mom and Dad had left when they went over the fells with the flocks. Hugh read it out loud: "Home by the longest night. Keep warm, and look after each other."

Ember's ears went up. "The longest night," she said softly. "That is Beacon Night. That is when the children of Candlemere carried the flame up Wardlow."

Hugh sat up very straight. Children had carried the flame!

Just then the fire gave a flicker, low and blue, like a candle in a draft. Grandma didn't hurry. She tucked the coals under their blanket of ash, and everyone said the banking words with her: "Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright."

Clara was loudest of all on the very last word. Then Grandma thanked the fire, the way you thank a friend, and it settled down like a cat on a cushion.

That night nobody went up to bed. Grandma spread a quilt on the hearth-rug, and everyone slept by the fire. Ember curled round Clara like a warm red scarf, her tail over Alfie's toes. In the hush, Hugh was the last to close his eyes. Somebody would have to carry a light all the way to Wardlow. But who?

Back came Clara with the rind in both hands. Ember ate it in one gulp. "I couldn't possibly have another," said Ember.

"All the fires in the Westering are one family," said Ember. "And one by one, the fires are falling asleep." Alfie counted the dark windows, one finger for each. "One, two, three, four, lots," said Ember.

Then the fire gave a flicker, low and blue. Everyone joined in with the old banking words: "Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright." And the fire settled down like a cat on a cushion.

Grandma spread a quilt on the hearth-rug, and everyone slept by the fire. Ember curled round Clara like a warm red scarf. Somebody would have to carry a light all the way to Wardlow. But who?
