## The shape

It is the Odyssey's shape: out into the world and home again. The lights of Candlemere are going out. A polite fox knocks at the window, and when every grown-up along the lane says "Not I," three ordinary children say "I'll help." They carry the last flame west along the Keepers' Way. They meet good hosts and bad hosts, and at every dark village they share the flame and ask for one stick of wood on the longest night. They get past a lonely giant by being polite and clever instead of strong, and then show him mercy. They almost forget home in a honey-sweet valley. They climb Blakefell, where the wind sings "stay and sleep," and hold on through the fog on Sedgefen. Then Hugh's one careless moment with the knotted winds blows them all the way back, close enough to see Grandma's window, and they choose to turn around. They follow a trail of small clues to the old keepers' stones and learn the truth about the last keeper. They tell the truth when it's hard. At the top of Wardlow, on the longest night, comes **the darkest hour**: the fire-bowl is empty, and the last flame goes out. They don't pretend. They say how they feel and hold hands. Then comes **the sudden joyful turn**: one light is climbing the hill below, then ten, then a hundred. Everyone they helped is coming, each carrying a lamp lit from theirs and one stick. The flame they gave away comes back to them. The beacon blazes, every hearth in the Westering brightens where it stands, and **the homecoming** follows. They are carried home asleep, known first by an old dog, tested by Grandma's secret question, wrapped in a scarf as long as the lane, and asked to tell their tale truly. Their names go on the lantern, under everyone who came before.

## The part sheets

### Part 1 · The Last Light · Candlemere

Chapters 1 to 5 · Brave · `Brave · between timid and reckless`

**The place.** Candlemere is a stone village round a still mere that holds every lit window upside down, and tonight its lamps go out one by one until only Grandma's hearth is left. Indoors it is inglenook, hearth-rug and a latch low enough for the eldest to lift; outside, the lane is hushed and dim, doors shut, neighbors yawny and grey. Then the Keepers' Way runs west past hedgerows and carved milestones into the first windy night on the road, and the part ends at dusk at the bolted doors of Ashcombe.

**Hosts and how they talk**

- **Grandma Ruth (the grandparent, keeper of the last hearth, Ch 1 to 3).** Warm, unhurried and a little twinkly, in short sentences and old sayings: "You've got your grandmother's tail." "Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright." "Nobody's too small to carry a light. But nobody carries it alone." Keeps Hild's secret: "That's not mine to tell." Never scolds, never worried. Every sentence with the grandparent as subject sits in a grandparent block.
- **Ember (the guest at the window, then the guide).** Quick, polite and a little grand. First words ever: "Good evening. I'm so sorry to bother you." Calls the children "my lot", fusses over her magnificent tail and wet paws, and can't count past four. Brings the news, never the answer. Grown-ups on the lane hear only fox yips.
- **Hild the wick-seller, with Dun (Ch 2 only).** Few words, all questions, comfortable silences. Asks the eldest alone, "How big must a person be to carry a lantern?", gives each child a pocket question, and gives the youngest a brass bell: "Ring it if you ever lose them." Understands Ember and doesn't say why.
- **Reeve Osric (Ch 2).** Kind, busy, always halfway to somewhere else: "We'll see to it tomorrow." Never mocked; he owns it himself in Ch 59.
- **The baker and the miller of Candlemere (unnamed, Ch 2).** Friendly and sorry, each with a real reason. The baker, floury and rushed: "Not I. The bread won't bake itself." The miller, slow and creaky, rubbing his knees: "Not I. These old knees won't climb Wardlow."
- **Will, the baker's boy (Ch 2).** Loud, quick and full of dares. Dashes into the dark with no light, is back in a minute, muddy, then proudly rings the youngest's bell all the way home. Helped, never laughed at.
- **Behind the door at Ashcombe (Ch 5).** Unseen and unheard in Part 1: a bolt sliding back and the smell of bread. Cuthbert speaks first in Ch 6.

**Modeled behavior.** When something needs doing, be the first to say "I'll help."

**The echo, and how it stays quiet.** Shapes and images only; story text never names a source. Ch 1 (Odyssey 1): the fox is let in and dried on the hearth-rug before anyone asks her news. Ch 2 (The Little Red Hen; Belling the Cat): "Not I" at every door; everyone agrees someone must go, nobody will. Reversed: nobody is shut out for it, Will gets a job, and the lane climbs Wardlow in Ch 54. Hild's bell stays a safety bell, never a bell for a cat. Faith on: Isaiah 6:8 only in Ch 2's faith note. Ch 3 (Odyssey 2): setting out as night comes on. Reversed: Telemachus slips away without telling his mother; these children go with the grandparent's yes, and a scarf is cast on (Penelope's weaving, uncommented). Ch 4 (Vesta's hearth): the banking words and a thank-you keep the flame alive. Ch 5 (the old law of guests): a courteous knock at dusk, Ember's greeting on a child's lips, bread behind the door.

**Tuesday: the mean made visible**

- *Too little:* Timid: the grown-ups along the lane saying "Not I" (the baker is too busy, the miller's knees are too stiff, Reeve Osric says "tomorrow"). Each has a real reason and stays likable; nobody is mocked.
- *Too much:* Reckless: Will the baker's boy runs into the dark with no light, and is back in a minute, muddy. He is helped straight away: the youngest gives him a hand and a job ("You carry the bell."). The eldest finds the middle way at the hearth: "I'll help."

**Breakfast set piece.** "Good evening. I'm so sorry to bother you." Letting the fox in (Ch 1). At breakfast one child is the fox: tap, tap, tap on the table, then the line in Ember's grandest voice. The youngest looks back at a grown-up ("She's cold!"), waits for the nod, and says "Come in, fox." The eldest lifts a pretend latch, and the fox shakes herself dry all over everyone. Swap parts and play it again.

**The clue.** Grandma and Hild can understand Ember; no other grown-up can (Ch 1 and 2). One name on the lantern's base is worn smooth, and Grandma says, "That's not mine to tell." (Ch 3). Ember smells someone she knows on the road ahead: "Someone else walked this road tonight. Someone I know." (Ch 3)

### Part 2 · The Shut Doors of Ashcombe · Ashcombe

Chapters 6 to 10 · Brave · `Brave · between timid and reckless`

**The place.** Ashcombe is a ring of stone cottages deep in a narrow valley under tall grey ash trees, with a small square in the middle and Cuthbert's cold forge at the bottom of the lane. Every door is bolted and every shutter shut, and the only sounds are a latch clicking and a whisper behind a window, so the whole village feels as if it is holding its breath. It is a hush, never a threat: tired lamps and grey faces peering out, until by Thursday the square smells of soup and is full of people laughing.

**Hosts and how they talk**

- **Cuthbert (the smith, Ch 6 to 10).** Old, slow and warm, with few words, like a man who talks to iron more than to people. He feeds first and asks after: "Eat. Questions keep." Never more than three short sentences at once. He calls the children "travelers", never by a word that needs a pronoun, and he stands with them at every door they knock on in Ch 8 and 9.
- **The reeve of Ashcombe (unnamed, Ch 8 and 9).** Tired and clipped, scared of the dark underneath, speaking round a half-open door: "Go home." He never shouts and is never mean. He is the too-little side grown up, and in Ch 9 he comes out last, with something for the pot.
- **The big boy of Ashcombe (unnamed, Ch 7 to 9).** Loud and quick, all dares: "Bet nobody can run across in the dark!" After he trips over the pot he goes quiet, then says yes when Alfie asks him to help carry it. Nobody laughs at him. In a one-child family he takes the sibling beats (bible §6).

- **Hild (not a host; the mentor, Ch 10 only).** Short questions and plain facts about herself: "Heavier than it looks." "What did your lantern lose?" She never tells a child what anything means.

### Part 3 · The Giant of Ramsden · Ramsden

Chapters 11 to 15 · Brave · `Brave · between timid and reckless`

**The place.** Ramsden is a high sheep valley between bare fells: stone walls, wet grass, and a wind off the tops that can turn to thunder fast. Its heart is Wistan's great roofed stone fold, dim and warm and smelling of straw, wool and warm milk, with one huge gate and forty-nine sheep and a lamb breathing all round. Outside, the hill is green and sunny by morning, and the whole valley feels like a place that was friendly once and went quiet because nobody visits any more.

**Hosts and how they talk**

- **Wistan.** Few words, deep and slow, and sad underneath. Short plain sentences: "Guests stay." "No." "Everybody goes. Nobody stays." He never shouts at the children and never touches them; his kindness leaks out sideways (warm milk, a blanket, a song to his sheep at dusk). His grumpiness is loneliness and being scared of the dark, and on Friday, beside the open gate, he says the most he has said all year: "The old wick-woman came every winter with a light. Last winter she didn't."

**Modeled behavior.** When something big scares you, take a lantern breath and use your polite words: "Please may I…?" Don't shove, and don't hide.
