# Grit & Grace: Story bible

*The rulebook behind every Grit & Grace story, for every season and every family. It builds on `story/canon.md` (the settled Season 1 world, cast and 60 chapters), on `story/strengths.md` (all 16 strengths: tags, phrases, gestures, ladders and if-then plans) and on `decisions.md`. All examples use the sample family: Hugh (7), Alfie (5) and Clara (3). This repo is public, so real children's names never appear here.*

---

## 1. What this is and how to use it

The **canon** says what happens in Season 1: who is in it, where they go, and all 60 chapters. The **bible** says how any chapter is written: what it must do, how it sounds, how the classical engine runs underneath, how a real family's children fill the hero slots, and what the safety checks catch. When Season 2 is drafted, the bible stays and a new canon is written for that season.

### Who uses it, and how

| Reader | When | What to read |
|---|---|---|
| **Claude, drafting a season** | Once per season, before Jon's review | All of it, plus `story/strengths.md` for the season's four strengths. Sections 2 to 4 shape every chapter. Sections 6 and 7 hold the cast and the world. Section 11 lists the steps and what goes in the review packet. |
| **Claude, the nightly layer** | Every night, for every family | Sections 6, 8 and 9, plus `story/strengths.md` for the tag, phrase and if-then plan of any strength a legend or special shows. The nightly layer fills slots and nothing else. The reviewed text stays exactly as written. |
| **Claude, the checker** | At production, and every night | Section 9 and every rule marked **Must** below. It runs as its own call, with its own instructions, never inside the call that wrote the text. |
| **Jon** | Once a season, in two short sittings | Section 2 (the chapter checklist), section 9 (safety) and section 11 (the review). The rest when a question comes up. |

### What wins when documents disagree

**`decisions.md` beats `canon.md`. `canon.md` beats `story/strengths.md` and this bible. On anything about a single strength (its tag, phrase, gesture, ladder, if-then plans, echoes), `strengths.md` beats this bible.** On everything else, this bible wins over `strengths.md`. If a draft finds a clash, it follows the higher document and lists the clash in the review packet for Jon. It never quietly edits the higher document to match the lower one.

### How the rules are worded

- **Must**: checked on every chapter and every personal part. A chapter or part that breaks it fails. The check is either a **plain test** (a count, a word list, a comparison with reviewed text) or a **judgment check** by a second model, which must write down its reason (section 9).
- **Should**: a judgment call for the reviewer. Breaking it needs a reason in the packet.
- **Reviewer-only**: a quality no check can test well (a warm voice, a joke that lands). Jon and the drafter look for it; the checker does not fail a chapter for it.
- **Never** in a rule takes the tag of the rule it sits in. "Never" on its own, with no tag, is a Must.
- **Flagged for Jon**: a call this bible makes that is not settled in `decisions.md` or the canon. Each one is marked where it appears, and all of them are collected in the review (section 11).

Where the canon already settles something, this bible restates it in a line and points to the canon section (for example, "canon §3"). It does not repeat the 60 chapters, the votes, the "I can…" ladders or the mockup mapping. Likewise it points to `story/strengths.md` for strength details instead of keeping its own copy.

## 2. The promise: what every chapter must do

Every chapter makes the family the same promise: **your own child does something good that matters, you laugh at least once, you get a question worth asking, and everyone falls asleep somewhere warm.** The canon's recipe (canon §3, "How every chapter teaches") is the source. Here it is as a checklist a reviewer can tick.

### The chapter checklist

**Top of the chapter**

- [ ] **Tag line** matches the canon word for word, for example `Keep Going · between giving up and stubborn` (for a strength the season's canon doesn't yet tag, `strengths.md`; section 4.1). *Must.*
- [ ] **Title, day and lead child** match the canon's chapter table. *Must.*
- [ ] **"Last time…"** in one sentence, whatever the gap since the last chapter. Chapter 1 has none. *Must.*
- [ ] **The opening block** (a legend, Grandma's reply on Mondays, and "Last time…", which on Mondays carries Saturday's trial as a memory) is 120 words or fewer in the chapter-book telling, and 2 pictures or fewer in the picture-book telling. If a legend would push it over, the legend waits for the next chapter; the grandparent's words are never trimmed to make room (section 8). *Must.*
- [ ] **The problem arrives in the first minute:** within 150 words after the opening block in the chapter-book telling, and by the second picture after it in the picture-book telling. *Must.*

**Middle of the chapter**

- [ ] **The mean made visible:** someone leans too little and someone leans too much. It is the main job on Tuesdays. On other nights one line is enough. *Tuesday: must. Other nights: should.*
- [ ] **Nobody is mocked.** Whoever went too far is helped or shown changing, in the same chapter or by the end of the part. *Must.*
- [ ] **The question in the story:** Hild asks her question out loud, or a child asks their pocket question when she's absent. Chapter 1 is the exception. Specials follow their own checklist (section 8). *Must.*
- [ ] **Pause & ask** sits in the middle (between 35% and 65% of the way through), as a why-question built on the story question. It names who answers first. Format below. *Must.*
- [ ] **The modeled behavior** is done by a child, concretely, with the week's phrase spoken and the gesture described. *Must.*
- [ ] **The lead child's key act changes what happens.** If the youngest child acts, the act is real and small, never a token. *Must.*
- [ ] **Every child in the family says or does something.** Nobody is scenery. *Should.*
- [ ] **A laugh,** usually Ember's. *Must.*
- [ ] **The classical echo** from the canon's echo column is present and quiet. *Should.*
- [ ] **The reading lines** (chapter-book telling): one "your line" for the eldest-slot reader and two or three big-print words or a short refrain for a Kindergartner (section 3, "Stretching a Kindergartner and a 2nd grader"). *Must.*
- [ ] **The day's jobs are done as the canon's chapter table marks them.** The source is each chapter's Marks column plus the key paragraph above the chapter tables (canon §4), not the weekday. The usual week is in canon §3, "The weekly rhythm": Monday has Grandma's reply and Saturday's trial as a memory; Tuesday has the full mean and sets up the if-then plan; Wednesday has the idea jar, the "remember when" and the vote; Thursday has no Hild, a pocket question aloud and Ember's plan reminder; Friday has the reward, the part's "Why do you think…?", the letter home and the trial announcement. The canon's exceptions win. In Season 1: Chapter 1 has no reply and no trial memory; Chapter 3 has no "remember when"; Chapters 26 and 56 have no reply (Chapter 31 has two); Chapters 25, 55 and 60 send no letter; Chapter 51 carries the jar and the vote; Chapter 53 has neither. Production step 2 (section 11) writes a job list for every chapter, and the checker reads that list. *Must.*

**End of the chapter**

- [ ] **The reward shown:** trust, honor, friendship, a welcome at the door, or help that comes back. No prizes. *Friday: must. Other nights: should.*
- [ ] **The last paragraph lands somewhere warm.** It shows every child safe, together and comforted: a held hand, Ember's tail, a roof, or sleep. The hook may ask about the goal (will the flame hold? who is behind the door?), never about anyone's safety. In the picture-book telling, the final picture is the comfort image. Full test in section 9, "The fear scale". *Must.*
- [ ] **The last page** is complete and in the right order (layout below). *Must.*
- [ ] **No narrator moral and no identity praise** anywhere in the chapter. *Must.*
- [ ] **Word count** is in range (section 3). *Must.*
- [ ] **Every picture is pinned.** Each picture in the picture-book telling is pinned to a paragraph of the chapter-book telling, and the youngest's key act and the Pause & ask each have a picture of their own (section 3, "The shared read"). *Must.*
- [ ] **The safety check** passes (section 9). *Must.*

### The four tricky items, with a good and a bad example

**1. The mean made visible**

> **Good** (Chapter 27, water rising over the stones):
> The water crept over the fortieth stone, then the forty-first. Alfie sat down on his. "Let's just wait here," he said. "Someone will come and fetch us." Hugh was already three stones ahead. "Come on! If we run, we'll beat it!" Ember looked at the water, then at Hugh, then at the water. "I'd rather not run on wet stones," she said. "My paws have opinions." Clara pointed up. "Tree."

It works because both pulls are real and felt from the inside. Both boys are likable. The joke lets the tension out. The youngest points toward the middle way, and the choice still belongs to the children.

> **Bad:**
> Alfie was being a scaredy-cat again and just sat there like a baby. Hugh, as usual, was being silly and rushing ahead. Luckily Clara was sensible.

It fails because the narrator judges and labels ("scaredy-cat", "silly"). It mocks a child ("like a baby"). It typecasts ("again", "as usual"). Nobody feels a pull; the scene just announces who is wrong.

**2. Pause & ask**

Every Pause & ask uses this block (the canon's Chapter 28 example, canon §8, is the model):

| Line | What it holds | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **In the story** | Hild's question, or the pocket question a child asked on the page | Quoted exactly as the character said it. Chapter 1 has no In the story line (the pocket questions start in Chapter 2). *Must.* |
| **Question** | "Why do you think…?" about a character's choice | 20 words or fewer. Asks why, not what happened. One short lead-in line may come first, as in Chapter 1: "Clara let a cold, wet fox into the house. Why do you think she did?" *Must.* |
| **Answers first** | The child who answers first | The turn child or the lead child, never automatically the eldest. When the question touches a hero child's own mistake, that child never answers first: a sibling does, or the grown-up in a one-child family. *Must.* |
| **If they're stuck** | Two easier prompts: one about the story, one about the child's own life | Never a yes/no question. *Must.* |
| **For the youngest** | One talk prompt, plus an optional do-it prompt | The talk prompt is one of three kinds: a finish-the-line ("Clara held out her…"), a what-or-where question about the picture ("Where is the little dragon?"), or a link to the child's own life ("Tell me about a time you felt lost."). The do-it prompt is a gesture, a sound, or pointing at the picture, and starts "Show me…", never "Can you…?". Used in both tellings. *Must.* |
| **Stretch** | One harder "why" for a 7-to-9-year-old | Optional. One line. Asked after the first answer. *Should.* |
| **For the grown-up** | A fixed line, the same in every block | "Whatever they say, say it back a little longer: 'Yes, she was scared, and she went anyway.' There's no wrong answer." *Must.* |

**Why-questions about a mistake.** The heroes are the listening children, so "Why did Clara say 'Not me'?" lands as "Why did you do that?", asked in front of the siblings. So when a question is about a mistake or failing by one of the family's children, it asks how that child felt, or what helped them put it right. It never asks why they did the wrong thing. Good: "How do you think Clara's tummy felt before she told?" or "What helped Hugh turn round?" Bad: "Why did Hugh untie the knots?" A why-question about a hero child's failing act fails the check. *Must.* (A why-question about a grown-up's or a creature's choice is fine, even a poor one: "Why do you think Wistan kept his gate shut?" teaches that grumpy people are often lonely.)

> **Good** (Chapter 28):
> *In the story:* Clara asks, "Who needs help?"
> *Question:* "Why do you think Alfie made his voice small and soft for the little dragon?"
> *Answers first:* Alfie (the turn child this week; canon §8's talk card: "Let Alfie answer before his big brother.")
> *If they're stuck:* "What would you say to someone who was lost?" · "When has your voice gone small? What did you do?"
> *For Clara:* "Clara held out her…" (let her finish: "hand!") · Do-it: "Show me the lantern breath, so gently a flame would only lean."
> *Stretch (for Hugh, after Alfie):* "Alfie's voice went small in Ashcombe too. How did remembering that help him tonight?"
> *For the grown-up:* "Whatever they say, say it back a little longer: 'Yes, she was scared, and she went anyway.' There's no wrong answer."

> **Bad:**
> "Was it good that Alfie was kind to the dragon?" (yes/no, and the answer is obvious)
> "What lesson did Alfie learn?" (a moral quiz)
> "Would YOU be brave like Alfie?" (pressure, and it asks the child to judge themselves)
> "Can you do the lantern breath?" (a yes/no question to a 3-year-old; use "Show me…")

*Flagged for Jon:* the canon's own N4 page (canon §8) still has "Has your voice ever gone small?" and "Can you do the lantern breath…?". Both are yes/no questions. The model above rewords them; the canon text is left as it is until Jon agrees.

**3. The reward shown**

> **Good** (Chapter 48): Clara puts her hand on her heart and tells Ebba she left the gate open. Ebba says, "Thank you for telling me," and hands her the salt-lick, the most important job there is: calling the oxen home.

The reward for honesty is trust, and trust means a real job. It comes straight after the true words, so a small child can see the link.

> **Bad:** "Ebba gave Clara a shiny gold coin for being such a good, honest girl."

It fails three ways: a prize, identity praise ("good, honest girl"), and a trade that teaches a child that honesty is paid work. Also bad: showing only the punishment ("Ned was sent to bed with no supper"). The canon's rule is virtue shown rewarded, not just vice punished (canon §4, rule 1).

**4. The last page**

The last page sits after the story text on screen and in print. It carries the canon's LastPage content (canon §8), in an order that winds the children down. The last thing read aloud at lights-out is calm, never a task.

| Order | Line | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"The end of Chapter N"** | Fixed label. |
| 2 | **Why do you think…?** | One question about tonight's chapter (Fridays: the part's question from the canon). Marked "Ask now, or save it for breakfast." The rules for why-questions about a mistake apply (item 2 above). |
| 3 | **What happened** | One or two sentences naming what a child did and felt. Never what anyone learned. |
| 4 | **Blessing** | The week's verse, faith toggle on only. |
| 5 | **Calm close** | One fixed line, the same every night: "Fire banked. Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright." It is the last line read aloud. |
| 6 | **Next time** | A small label, not read aloud: the next chapter's number and title. Never "tomorrow night": chapters advance by reading. |
| 7 | **For the morning** | Shown under a "For the morning" heading, not read aloud at lights-out, and repeated on the morning screen and the fridge card. **Missions:** one per child, sized to their age band, doable tomorrow, tied to the week's modeled behavior. **Tell it back:** one idea for tomorrow: who tells whom, and which bit. |

All seven lines are *Must.*

> **Good** (Chapter 28): Why do you think…? "Why do you think the little dragon stopped crying?" (ask now, or save it for breakfast) · What happened: "Alfie's voice was small, and he used it anyway. Clara held out her hand, and waited, and the little dragon stopped crying and fell asleep against Ember's magnificent tail." · Blessing (faith on) · "Fire banked. Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright." · For the morning: Missions: Hugh: "Pick one hard thing and do just the first small part." Alfie: "When something's tricky, say 'not yet' and try a new way." Clara: "Try one more time before asking for help." Tell it back: "At breakfast, ask Clara to tell Grandma about the little dragon."

> **Bad:** "And so Alfie learned that being kind is the most important thing of all. Remember, kids: always help those in need! Will the fen-monster get the little dragon tomorrow?"

It fails on the moral ("learned that"), the lecture ("Remember, kids"), and the threat hook.

*Flagged for Jon:* the canon's recipe (canon §3, step 7) and its LastPage screen (canon §8, N6) put the missions and "tell it back" before the blessing, as part of what is read at lights-out. This bible keeps them on the page but moves them under "For the morning", after the calm close. The canon text is unchanged until Jon agrees.

**The Wednesday vote, calmly.** The vote comes at the end of Wednesday's chapter (canon §3), which is the last thing before sleep. A vote with a winner and a loser is a likely bedtime squabble, so: both options are read in the same quiet voice, with no "Which is more exciting?"; each child votes by whispering or by putting a hand on the choice, not by shouting; and the tie-break is the canon's (the turn child picks). The canon already lets a vote wait ("If Wednesday is missed, the vote waits too"), so the parent may also take the vote at breakfast; Thursday's chapter opens the same way. *Should.* *Flagged for Jon:* the breakfast vote is new. It needs a "Vote at breakfast" button on the vote screen (N5).

## 3. Voice and style

### The narrator

The house voice is **a warm grandparent telling a story by the fire.** On the surface it is modern and funny: quick, clear, a joke on most pages, and children who sound like real children. Underneath it is old: the rhythm of old tales, good manners, things done properly, the rule of three, real old words. The narrator loves every character, the grumpy ones most of all.

Each rule in this section carries its tag: *Must* (checked), *Should* (reviewer's call) or *Reviewer-only* (section 1).

The narrator does:
- **Tell it straight.** What happened, what they said, what they did. Feelings show in bodies: knees shake, a tummy goes wobbly, ears go flat. *Reviewer-only.*
- **Notice small true things.** "He noticed it, the way you notice rain." *Reviewer-only.*
- **Share a feeling with "you"** ("the way you look at an old friend"), at most twice a chapter in the narration (dialogue doesn't count), and never to give an instruction. *Should.*
- **Let silence work.** "Nobody answered." "Hugh waited." *Reviewer-only.*

The narrator never lectures (*Must*: the preaching check in section 9), never tells the listener how to feel, as in "Wasn't that wonderful?" (*Must*: a question from the narrator to the listener fails), never explains a joke and never winks at grown-ups at a child's expense (*Reviewer-only*).

### Read-aloud rhythm

- **Sentence length.** Chapter-book telling: an average of 9 to 14 words (*Should*), no sentence over 28 words (*Must*), and at least one very short sentence (4 words or fewer) on every page (*Should*). Picture-book telling: 4 to 12 words a sentence (*Should*), 15 at most (*Must*).
- **Paragraphs.** Four sentences at most (*Should*). One speaker per paragraph (*Must*).
- **The rule of three.** Three knocks, three tries, three lights. Old tales run on it, and children feel it coming. *Reviewer-only.*
- **Sound words.** One to three a chapter, in italics, easy to perform: *Tap. Tap. Tap.* *Pfft.* *Plip.* *Honk.* (*Should*). Never a scary sound: no screams, no growls near a child (*Must*).
- **A join-in line.** Every chapter has one line the children can say with the reader: the week's phrase, Ember's "One, two, three, four, lots!", or the banking words. Put a natural pause before it. It is marked in the production text so the checker can find it. *Must.*
- **Page turns.** The chapter-book telling runs about six pages. Each page ends on a small pull forward: a knock, a question, a sound. *Should.*

**House refrains** (the canon settles them; use them exactly, *Must*):

| Refrain | Who says it | When |
|---|---|---|
| "Lantern up!" | Any child | Brave weeks: doing it scared. |
| "Not yet… keep going!" | Any child | Keep Going weeks: hands climb an invisible ladder. |
| "Oops… up again!" | Any child | Bounce Back weeks: crouch small, spring up. |
| "Hand on heart: I'll say what's true." | Any child | Truth-Teller weeks: one hand flat on the heart. |
| "Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright." | Grandma first, then the children | The banking words, whenever a fire is settled for the night. |
| "Good evening. I'm so sorry to bother you." | Ember | Her first words ever; the Part 1 set piece. |
| "One, two, three, four, lots." | Ember | Whenever anyone counts. |
| "The way on is yours." | Ember | Whenever a child asks her what to do. |
| "That's not mine to tell." | Grandma, Ember | Someone else's story, left for them to tell. |
| "…and the flame hopped across." | Narrator | Every time a light is given. |

### Dialogue

- Use **"said"** almost always. "Asked", "whispered" and "called" when they matter. No "exclaimed", "retorted" or piles of adverbs. *Should.*
- **Keep lines short.** For new lines: a 3-to-4-year-old's line is 8 words or fewer, a 5-to-6-year-old's 12 or fewer, and a 7-to-9-year-old's 20 or fewer. One longer line a chapter is allowed when it is the child's key act. A grown-up may say three sentences at once, never a speech. *Should.* Lines quoted from the canon are exempt (Clara's "We walked a long way, and the giant was nice." in Chapter 57 is 10 words, and stays).
- **Children sound like children** ("Me do it!", "Not me!"). *Reviewer-only.* No baby-talk spellings. *Should.*
- **Grown-ups are courteous** in an old, warm way: "please", "thank you", "sir". *Reviewer-only.*
- **Each regular has a voice.** Ember: polite, a little grand, "my lot", "my magnificent tail". Hild: mostly short questions; plain facts about herself; never a lesson (section 4.5). Grandma: warm, full of old sayings. Reeve Osric: "We'll see to it tomorrow." Wistan: few words, deep and sad. Edith: gruff, short sentences that soften. *Reviewer-only.*
- **No character states the moral,** and nobody explains a lesson to anybody else. *Must* (the preaching check).

### Humor

- **A laugh in every chapter** (canon §4, rule 20), usually Ember's: counting to "lots", the sneeze when she tries to fib, her vanity about her tail, sausages and cheese rinds, wet paws, ears blown inside out. *Must* (a judgment check: the checker names the laugh line).
- **Gentle slapstick:** a wet fox shaking herself dry all over Alfie, boots on the wrong feet, feathers snowing out of a bed, a goose honking at exactly the wrong moment. Nobody is hurt and nobody is humiliated. *Reviewer-only.*
- **Funny situations:** a whole valley too sleepy to finish a sentence, a reeve who keeps saying "tomorrow", an old dog who never gets up for anyone. *Reviewer-only.*
- **A child may be funny. A child is never the butt of the joke.** Nobody in the story laughs at a child's mistake unless the child laughs first (Clara, finding her bow in the hawthorn: "Oops… up again!"). *Must* (the shaming check).
- **One grown-up wink per chapter at most,** and only a kind one. *Reviewer-only.*
- **Never:** sarcasm aimed at a child, put-downs, toilet or gross-out jokes (mud and a wet fox are the limit), or jokes about looks, bodies, accents, fussy eating or being small. *Must.*

### Word choice for each telling

| | Picture-book telling | Chapter-book telling |
|---|---|---|
| **Usually for** | Ages 3 to 4 | Ages 5 to 9 (read aloud to 5-year-olds) |
| **Length** | 300 to 450 words (*Must*) over 8 to 12 pictures (*Must*), usually 10 to 12. One to three short sentences a picture, about 20 to 40 words (*Should*); up to two pictures may carry four. | About 900 to 1,100 words (*Must*), about 7 minutes aloud, about 6 pages |
| **Sentences** | 4 to 12 words (*Should*), 15 at most (*Must*) | Average 9 to 14 words (*Should*), 28 at most (*Must*) |
| **Rich words** | 1, shown in the picture and said twice (*Should*) | 3 to 5 (*Must*), with the rules below |
| **Must keep** | The same events, the same choices, the Pause & ask (simplified), the youngest child's act, the week's phrase (*Must*) | Everything on the checklist |
| **Dialogue** | One speaker a picture at most (*Should*) | As above |
| **Sound words** | 1 or 2 (*Should*) | 1 to 3 (*Should*) |
| **Classical echo** | The shape only (*Should*) | A shape, an image or a line (*Should*) |

**The picture-book numbers add up.** Ten pictures of three 10-word sentences make 300 words. Twelve pictures of 35 words make 420. Fewer than ten pictures will usually need a four-sentence picture or two to reach 300. *Flagged for Jon:* the canon's "one or two lines each" (canon §3, Season shape) can't reach 300 words with sentences of 12 words or fewer. This bible uses "one to three short sentences a picture" and leaves the canon's wording for Jon to change.

The parent chooses each child's telling in settings. The default at age 5 is the chapter-book telling, read aloud (canon §8, Settings).

### The shared read

The canon's promise is "One story for all three" (canon §8, Welcome), but Hugh and Alfie are on the chapter-book telling and Clara is on the picture-book telling. A parent reading aloud can read only one text. So:

- **When children on different tellings are read to together, the reader reads the chapter-book telling.** This is the normal night for most families. *Must* (the app opens that telling).
- **The pictures ride along.** Each of the 8 to 12 picture-book pictures is pinned to one paragraph of the chapter-book telling, and shows beside it on screen and in print. *Must.*
- **The youngest's key act always has its own picture,** pinned to the paragraph where it happens. *Must.*
- **The "For the youngest" prompt sits at its picture,** so the reader asks the youngest at the moment they can see it. The Pause & ask has its own picture too. *Must.*
- **The picture-book telling on its own** is for reading to the youngest alone, for the fold-a-book, and for the grandparent's large-text read-along page.

The checker tests that every picture is pinned to a paragraph, and that the youngest's act and the Pause & ask each have one.

### Stretching a Kindergartner and a 2nd grader

`decisions.md` asks that the stories and reading levels work for a Kindergartner and a 2nd grader who like a challenge. Listening words alone don't do that. Each chapter-book chapter gives both children something to read themselves, and gives everyone words they can use tomorrow.

- **"Your line" for the eldest-slot reader.** Every chapter-book chapter has one line for a reader in the eldest slot to read aloud: 1 to 3 sentences at about a 2nd-grade level (short, sound-it-out words, no sentence over 12 words). It is something in the story that is read: a line of a letter, a sign, a carving, or Ember's words. It is set in normal sentence case, never all capitals: capitals hide the word shapes a young reader is learning. (A carving may be drawn in capitals in the picture, with the readable line printed beside it.) The page marks it so the parent can hand the reading over. Example: *Your line:* "Keep the light, and pass it on." *Must.*
- **Big-print words for a Kindergartner.** Every chapter has two or three big-print words or a short refrain a Kindergartner can read: simple sound-it-out words or common sight words ("Not me!", "Up! Up!", "Lots!"). The join-in line counts when it is short enough. *Must.*
- **Rich words: 3 to 5 in every chapter-book chapter.** At least two of them are everyday words a child can use tomorrow: *weary, courteous, stubborn, gentle, grumble, peer, homesick, steady.* Place and setting words (*tallow, sedge, fen, reeve, drove, inglenook*) are welcome in the story, but they don't count toward the 3 to 5. *Must.*
- **Each rich word is met close together.** It appears at least twice in its own chapter and comes back at least once more within the same part. Each is explained by context in the same or the next sentence, never by a dictionary definition, and no paragraph has more than one new one. Example: "Hugh set the lantern down in the **lee** of the milestone, on the side the wind couldn't reach." *Must.*
- **The week's two words go home.** Each part picks two of its everyday rich words for the fridge card and the talk cards. *Must.*
- **Something to count.** Alfie's counts are real numbers the listener can count along with. *Should.*
- **A harder why.** The Stretch line in Pause & ask. *Should.*
- **No talking down.** No narrator "little ones", no "Can you believe it, kids?", no explaining a feeling the page has already shown. Trust children with big things: loneliness, being wrong, mercy, a grown-up who failed and says so. *Reviewer-only* (the word list "little ones", "kids" in narration is *Must*).

### What never appears

Everything in this table is *Must.*

| Never | Examples | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Brands and media characters** | Any product, shop, film, TV or game character | The underlying thing, made world-true (section 8, idea jar). |
| **Screens and modern machines** | Phones, tablets, TVs, cars, trains, electric light | Letters, lanterns, carts, geese carrying news. |
| **Modern slang** | "awesome", "cool" as praise, "literally", "OMG", "dude", "epic", "whatever" | Plain warm words: "wonderful", "well done", "oh my". ("Okay" is fine in a child's mouth.) |
| **Sarcasm aimed at a child** | "Oh, great job, genius." | Say the true thing kindly, or say nothing. |
| **Weapons used on anyone** | Swords, bows, spears, fists | Tools only: a smith's hammer, a shepherd's crook, a walking stick. |

## 4. The classical engine

The engine runs underneath the story. The surface stays warm, fun and modern (`decisions.md`). A child should feel the engine, never hear it explained.

### 4.1 The golden mean

**Virtue is a habit, and it sits between two failings: too little and too much.** Every chapter is tagged with its strength and both failings, in this exact format (*Must*, character for character):

`Strength · between too-little and too-much`

**Where the tags live.** The Season 1 tags are settled word for word (canon §3), and `strengths.md` copies them. **Tags, phrases, gestures, ladders and default if-then plans for all 16 strengths live in `story/strengths.md`** ("The 16 at a glance"). For those items it ranks below the canon and above this bible (section 1). The Seasons 2 to 4 pairs there are working pairs until Jon approves them (`strengths.md`, flag 2); until then the checker takes Seasons 2 to 4 tags from `strengths.md` only, and copies them exactly. This bible keeps no second copy. When a season's own canon is written and approved, it wins for that season.

Legends and specials can carry any of the 16 tags, because a spotted deed shows whatever strength it shows (canon §8, Three legends). The nightly layer takes the tag from `strengths.md`.

**The Season 1 pairs, and what each side looks like** (from canon §3):

| Strength | Tag | What each side looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Brave | `Brave · between timid and reckless` | Hiding from what's right because you're scared / rushing in without thinking. |
| Keep Going | `Keep Going · between giving up and stubborn` | Quitting at the first wobble / doing the same thing over and over, never resting, never asking for help. |
| Bounce Back | `Bounce Back · between falling apart and shrugging it off` | "I ruin everything," and staying down / pretending it didn't happen and learning nothing. |
| Truth-Teller | `Truth-Teller · between fibbing and blurting` (Part 12: `between making it bigger and making it smaller`) | Hiding, "not me", making things up / saying true things unkindly, tattling, bragging. |

**Three things about the mean that keep it honest.**

1. **Most of these pairs are ours, built on Aristotle's method.** He named the two failings himself for only a few: courage (Brave), truthfulness (the Part 12 Truth-Teller pair), and near matches for Fair Play, Pause Button and Enough. The canon's Keep Going words also follow his. Every other pair is our own, and the notes say so (`strengths.md`, rule 12 and flag 6). Never write "Aristotle said kindness is between…".
2. **Some things have no middle.** Lying to hide a wrong, taking what isn't yours and cruelty are never "a small amount" of a virtue, and there is no right amount of any of them. The too-much side is always a different fault, such as unkindness, bossiness, pride or rigidity. It is never too much truth, too much fairness or too much kindness. So a Tuesday never shows a character who is "too honest": the blurter's fault is unkindness, not truth. *Must* (a judgment check).
3. **The middle isn't halfway.** It depends on the child and the moment. A 3-year-old's brave (knocking while holding a hand) is not a 7-year-old's (going back to the giant's gate). The age-band versions of each key act (section 6) are how the story keeps the middle in the right place for each child.

**How the mean shows on the page**

- **Tuesday is the full version** (canon §3, the part engine table lists every Season 1 pair). One character leans too little and one leans too much, and the children feel the pull of both.
- **Other nights get a light touch:** one line where a pull is felt, often inside the lead child ("Hugh wanted to run").
- **Both failings belong to likable people.** Whoever leans too far is helped or shown changing (Old Alric's cart rolls when it's pushed a new way; the puffing walker gets up and tries the cairn trick).
- **Nobody is typecast.** Across a season, the eldest and the middle child each lean both ways at least once. The youngest leans at least once and is never only the baby who needs rescuing.
- **It's often funny,** and never mocking.
- **The middle way is found by a child,** usually with the week's phrase, never announced by the narrator.

### 4.2 Right and wrong are real

- **Wrong is named plainly by the one who did it:** "I untied them." "I did it." Nobody pretends it didn't matter, and nobody piles on.
- **Mistakes are owned, then they're over** (canon §4, rule 1). No sulking punishment, no bringing it up again as a weapon.
- **Mercy is not pretending.** Wistan shut the gate, and that was wrong. Hugh goes back with a light anyway. Mercy sees the wrong and chooses kindness.
- **The ending never says "everyone was right in their own way"** when someone wasn't. Sides can both have reasons, and there is still a right thing to do.
- **Every child makes at least one real mistake a season and owns it** (Hugh's knots, Alfie's giggle, Clara's gate). So do grown-ups (Hild, Reeve Osric).

### 4.3 Virtue rewarded with trust, honor and friendship, not prizes

Good acts earn what good acts earn in real life. Research on young children supports this: a story that rewards honesty raised truth-telling, and stories that only punish lying did not (`research-findings.md`, row 8). Material rewards for helping reduced later helping in toddlers, while praise did not (row 11).

| Reward | What it looks like | Season 1 example |
|---|---|---|
| **Trust** | A real job, a key, being believed | Ebba gives Clara the salt-lick; Ebba trusts Alfie to shut the gate. |
| **Honor** | A name remembered, a place at the table, a story told of you | Names scratched on the lantern; the children ask Hild to lay the last stick. |
| **Friendship** | A grumpy host who softens, a promise, help that comes back | Wistan's log; Edith's laugh; the line of lights up Wardlow. |
| **Welcome** | A door opened, a meal before questions | Cuthbert feeds them before asking a thing. |

**Rules.** No gold, medals, stars, sweets or treasure as a reward. Food at a welcome is hospitality, given before any deed, never payment for one. A gift may come as friendship (Wistan's fleece), never as payment for being good, and gifts are usually useful and often given on (Clara pulls the fleece into tufts for everyone's ears). Praise in the story is for effort and choices ("You tried a new way!"), never for identity ("brave girl", "good boy").

### 4.4 A classical echo in every chapter

Every chapter quietly retells a motif from Homer, Aesop, fairy tales, Arthurian legend, the Stoics, or Scripture (faith toggle on). Season 1's spine is the Odyssey: the journey there and home again (canon §4).

**How to echo quietly.** Use one of three moves:
- **A shape:** the plot moves the way the old story moves (a stranger welcomed before questions; slipping out under the sheep).
- **An image:** one picture from the old story (ears stopped with fleece; a scarf knitted row by row).
- **A line:** a phrase that rhymes with an old one ("A light that's taken will not catch").

**Rules.**
- **Never name the source in story text.** No "just like Odysseus", no lesson about Homer. A grown-up may notice; a child doesn't need to.
- **Reverse what doesn't fit.** Where the old hero wins by force, a lie or a boast, our children win by courtesy, cleverness and truth (canon: "No blinding and no lie"; "Reverses Odysseus's boast").
- **Soften the ending, keep the shape.** No one is eaten, killed or punished cruelly. The old tale's meaning must survive the softening; if it can't, choose another tale.
- **When the old tale pays virtue in treasure, the retelling pays it in trust, friendship or honor.** Jewels, gold coins and golden axes become a welcome, a friend, a real job or being believed (section 4.3). *Must.*
- **Grown-up notes may name the source.** The Sunday plan and talk cards can say "This week echoes the Sirens" for parents who want it. *(Flagged for Jon.)*

**The source shelf.** "Used" means Season 1 already uses it (see the canon's echo columns). Everything else is on the shelf for later seasons, specials and library retellings.

### 4.5 The Socratic mentor: Hild

Hild is settled in the canon (canon §1, "The mentor"). Her rules:

- **She never hands over the answer.** Hild never tells a child what to do, what something means, or what they should learn. Her teaching lines are always questions. When a child asks "What should we do?", she answers with a question: "What do you think?" *Must* (a judgment check).
- **She may say plain things.** She may state short plain facts about herself or the world ("Heavier than it looks," Chapter 10), give a practical safety instruction (the bell: "Ring it if you ever lose them," Chapter 2), and tell her own story truthfully when it is hers to tell (her confession in Chapter 45: "A keeper carves the flame in when the work is handed on… Mine never was."). None of these is a lesson.
- **She waits,** and lets the silence work. After the children act, she asks why they did it.
- **When she appears** (Season 1): Chapters 2, 10, 21, 35, 45, 55 and 60, on Mondays, Tuesdays or Fridays, near the start of a part or after the danger. **Never on a Thursday** (the test night belongs to the children) and never in the middle of a crisis. Absent for all of Parts 3, 4, 6, 8 and 10.
- **Pocket questions.** In Chapter 2 she gives each child one question to ask when she isn't there. Hugh: "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?" Alfie: "What else could we try?" Clara: "Who needs help?" In every chapter she misses, a child asks their own pocket question aloud. (With more or fewer than three children, see section 6.)
- **How the questions move.** First Hild asks. Then each child asks their own pocket question. By Part 10 the children ask each other Hild's questions. Every night, the Pause & ask hands the same kind of question to the parent, who asks it the way Hild would, without trying. That is the Lantern Loop working: the question moves from the mentor into the child.
- **She is not a wizard, a guide or a recruiter.** She sells wicks and asks one question. She is never the one who solves anything.
- **Later seasons.** Hild remains the mentor unless a season's canon names another. Any mentor follows these same rules.

### 4.6 Tolkien's influence, and staying original

**What we take from Tolkien** (`decisions.md`):
- **Ordinary small folk** showing quiet courage. Nobody is chosen; they go because someone has to.
- **Hospitality:** good hosts feed first and ask later; good guests knock, say thank you, share their light and leave something behind (canon §4, rule 4).
- **Loyalty between friends:** siblings who stand beside each other ("I'll stand with you").
- **Mercy:** the grumpy giant is shown mercy and repays it.
- **The sudden joyful turn at the darkest moment:** the flame goes out on Wardlow, and then one small light is climbing the hill.

**What we never take.** No Tolkien names, places or plots. The canon keeps the full list of banned names and near-copies (canon §2, "Naming rules"). The images to avoid, from the canon:

- no watch-tower on a hill (Wardlow is a watch hill, never a tower)
- no burial mounds or barrows
- no lights in marsh water
- no grasping old willow
- no riddle game in the dark
- no beacons lit hill to hill (one beacon only)
- no "the road goes ever on" phrasing

This bible proposes one more, as a candidate *(flagged for Jon)*:

- no door that opens to a spoken password or riddle answer (too close to the gate of Moria, "Speak, friend, and enter")

The Season 3 seed (canon §6) has library doors that "open only to good questions, set as old English riddles". The canon is unchanged. The Season 3 outline sheet (section 11) carries it as a decision for Jon, with one possible fix: the librarian answers only good questions, and the doors stay ordinary doors.

Every new name gets the canon's name check before it goes into a season: a Tolkien index check and a real-place search.

## 5. The Lantern Loop in the story

The Lantern Loop is the teaching method (`decisions.md`): **meet it, name it, ask why, plan, get spotted, remember when.** Here is where each step lands in the story and the week. The weekly rhythm itself is settled in canon §3.

| Step | What it looks like on the page | Where it lands in the week |
|---|---|---|
| **Meet it** | The child is the hero. The strength is met in a chapter where their own act changes what happens. | Every chapter, Monday to Friday. Monday brings a new place and a new problem. |
| **Name it** | The week's phrase is spoken and the gesture described, at the moment of the modeled behavior. | Every chapter. The Sunday plan and the fridge card name it off the page. |
| **Ask why** | The story question (Hild's, or a pocket question), then Pause & ask to the listener. The last page asks one "Why do you think…?". | Every chapter, in the middle. Friday asks the part's big why (canon §4, part engine table). |
| **Plan** | An if-then plan in the child's words. | Built on Tuesday after reading (screen V4). Ember reminds that child of it inside Thursday's chapter. |
| **Spotted** | A real deed, logged by a grown-up, told as a legend. | Opens any chapter, once approved (section 8). |
| **Remember when** | Ember whispers "Remember when…?" and hands a child back their own brave moment. | Every Wednesday (canon §4 marks each one "RW"), plus the big one in Chapter 53. |

### The turn child

Each week one child is **"the child whose turn it is"**: they build the if-then plan on Tuesday, Ember reminds them of it on Thursday, and they break ties in the vote. **The turn passes in a fixed order, eldest, then youngest, then any middle children from oldest down, so every child gets the same number of weeks.** Twelve parts divide evenly for 1, 2, 3 or 4 children. For the sample family that is Hugh, Clara, Alfie, repeating, which puts Part 6 with Alfie, as the mockup's if-then screen shows. The Thursday chapter always gives the turn child a moment to use their plan, even when another child leads it. *(Flagged for Jon: the canon names the turn child but not how the turn passes.)*

### The if-then plan

- **Format:** "If [a moment the child can notice], then I [one thing they can do]." One action for a 3-year-old (`strengths.md`, rule 9). *Must.*
- **Written by the child,** with the parent's help, after Tuesday's chapter. The app offers the default plan for the child's rung from `strengths.md` (each strength's ladder has an if-then plan for every rung). For example, Alfie on the Keep Going Middle rung: "If I want to quit, then I say 'not yet' and try a new way." (The canon's StrengthDetail page, canon §8, shows the Keep Going plans only.)
- **The child's own words are personal text.** They are quoted in the story only if they pass the checker and fit the one-action shape; otherwise the story uses the rung's default plan (section 8, "Each kind of personal content"). A plan like "If Alfie takes my blocks, then I hit him" is never read aloud; the parent sees a short note, and the default runs. *Must.*
- **Ember's Thursday reminder is a join-in.** Ember says the "if" half. The text then shows a pause cue for the reader, so the listening child can say the "then" half first. The story child's line comes after the pause and matches the plan. *Must.*
  > Ember nudged Alfie with her nose. "Your plan," she whispered. "If you want to quit…"
  > *(Pause. Let Alfie say it.)*
  > Alfie took a breath. "Then I say 'not yet'," he said, "and try a new way."
- **No plan made?** Ember uses the rung's default plan from `strengths.md`. The reminder never scolds and never mentions that the plan was missing.

### Spotted, then a legend

A grown-up spots a real deed in the day and logs it. It becomes a legend: a line or two, told true and not bigger, tagged with the strength it shows, that opens a chapter ("In the village of Candlemere they still tell of…"). The parent approves the exact line before it's used. Full rules in section 8.

### Remember when

## 6. Characters as slots

The product fills the hero slots with each family's own children: **1 to 4 children, ages 3 to 9, any gender, any look from the avatar builder.** The canon is written for three children in three slots: eldest, middle and youngest (canon §1). This section says how the story bends to fit every other family, without changing the plot.

### What comes from the family, and what never does

The family's settings supply: each child's **first name** (and nickname, if set), **age**, **pronouns**, **drawn look** from the avatar builder, their **little details** (favorites), and what the children call their **grandparent** and their **parents**. That's all.

**Never invent real-life facts about the family.** No made-up pets, friends, schools, homes, jobs, hobbies, health, history or family events. If the family didn't enter it or say it (in a legend, an idea, a voice note or a special), the story doesn't know it.

### Names and pronouns

- **Names** are used exactly as entered. A nickname is used only if the family set one. The avatar builder's pronunciation hint (if given) goes to the voice layer, not into the text. *Must.*
- **Names are checked once, at setup.** Every child's and grandparent's name goes through the personal-data and never-appears checks (section 9) when it is entered. If one fails (a celebrity's name, a brand, a joke word), the app asks the parent before it is used. *Must.*
- **A family name that matches a cast name.** If a child's or grandparent's name matches a recurring or one-part cast member, that cast member uses a reviewed alternate for this family: Will becomes Wilf, Ned becomes Ted, Edith becomes Eda. Ember always stays Ember: a child named Ember is called by their nickname in the story, and the app asks the parent to set one. Every named cast member gets a reviewed alternate at production (section 11, step 6). *Must.*
- **The master text is written with the sample names** (for Jon to read). Each child's line is tied to its slot, so a straight swap works (the same method `tools/build-private.py` uses for Jon's private copy).

### Reviewed variant blocks: pronouns and the grandparent

Some settings change more than a name. "They" changes verbs ("he is" becomes "they are"). A grandfather changes "she" and "her" across dozens of lines. Two grandparents need plurals. Nan and a "remembered" grandparent need different lines. **None of this is written by the nightly layer.** Every such change is a reviewed variant block, drafted at production (section 11, step 6):

- **The child's pronoun:** she, he, or they. The "they" version uses the child's name a little more often, so every sentence stays clear to a listener. That choice is made once, in the reviewed block.
- **The grandparent:** she, he or they; one grandparent or two ("both can stand at the hearth", canon §1).
- **Nan** (no grandparent set) and **remembered** (canon §1).

The nightly layer only picks blocks. **Slot integrity means the text is identical to the selected variant blocks,** with only the names and slots filled (section 9). *Must.*

**Real-life lines for families without a living grandparent.** Some lines off the page assume a grandparent to talk to: "ask Clara to tell Grandma" on the last page, the Part 12 trial "Tell Grandma one part of the story", and "Send tonight's picture to Grandma Ruth". For a family whose grandparent is marked "remembered", or who set no grandparent, these become "tell a grown-up who loves you", and the share-to-grandparent prompt is hidden. The story character (Nan) is unchanged. *Must.*

### Access: every child can do the key act

The text mentions only what the avatar builder captured: hair, glasses, freckles, clothes and colors, a wheelchair, hearing aids. **At most one or two touches a chapter,** lightly ("Alfie pushed his glasses up his nose"). *Must.* **The text never describes skin color;** the pictures show it. The text never comments on body size and never praises looks ("pretty", "handsome"). *Must.*

**A child who uses a wheelchair, hearing aids, a cane, signs or a talker device is written as capable.** The equipment is ordinary, like glasses. It is never explained, never pitied, never cured, never a magic power, and never the problem the chapter solves. The chair is "her chair" (or "his wheels", if the family chose that wording). Hearing aids are "his hearing aids". *Must.*

**The rule: every key act that depends on seeing, hearing, walking, climbing or speaking has a version with the same act and the same result.** The child does the same job, shows the same strength and changes what happens in the same way. The season draft includes these versions (section 11, step 6). *Must.*

- **Speaking.** Every "say" act and every "say" rung on a ladder can be met by speech, signs, pointing or a talker device. The text says which ("Clara signed, *I did it*." "Alfie pressed his talker: *Can we start again?*"). Ember understands signs and talkers, because she understands children.

## 7. The world

The settled world is in canon §1 (what is dimming the lights) and canon §2 (Candlemere, the Westering, the gazetteer and the naming rules). This section adds the everyday texture a drafter needs, and never changes what the canon says.

**How it should feel:** grounded, old and cozy, like the place your great-grandmother grew up. Hearths and inglenooks. Hedgerows full of blackberries. Mill streams and stepping stones. Wet fells with sheep on them. Every night ends somewhere warm.

### A walk through Candlemere

Candlemere sits at the eastern edge of the Westering. The land rolls away west from it toward the sunset, and Wardlow is far off at the western edge.

- **The mere.** Walk down the lane and the village opens round a still, round lake. On a calm night it holds every lit window upside down, like a second village underwater. That's the name: *candle-mere*. Now that the lights are going out, the mere is nearly dark.
- **The Moot Hall.** The village meeting hall, with a lamp over the door. Its lamp is the first we see go out, in Chapter 1. Old Brock, the Moot Hall dog, lies on the step and never gets up for anyone. Every autumn Hild sets out her tray of needles, thread and wicks by this door. The village meeting in Chapter 59 happens inside.
- **Grandma's cottage.** Stone walls, a slate roof, a big inglenook round the hearth, a hearth-rug a wet fox can shake herself dry on, and a window a fox can tap on. The parents' goodbye note stands on the mantel. The latch is low enough for the eldest to lift. It's the last light in Candlemere, because Grandma banks her fire every night the old way.
- **The mill.** On the mill stream, with a wheel that turns when the water runs. The miller's knees are too stiff to walk to Wardlow (Chapter 2).
- **The bakehouse.** Where the village bakes its bread. The baker is too busy to go (Chapter 2). Will, the baker's boy, runs off into the dark with no light and comes back muddy.
- **The green.** Open grass in the middle of things, where the village gathers, and where the Beacon Feast is held in Chapter 60 *(flagged for Jon: the canon doesn't say where the feast is held)*.
- **The lane.** Stone cottages door to door, where the children knock in Chapter 2 and hear "Not I" at every one.

### The Westering and the Keepers' Way

**The Westering** is the whole land: hedgerows and holloways, fells and fens, old roads marked with milestones. **The Swale** runs west from Candlemere almost to Wardlow. **The Keepers' Way** is the old road the keepers walked, with a carved lantern on every seventh milestone so they could find their way.

Season 1's road, in order: **Candlemere** → **Ashcombe** (a valley village of shut doors) → **Ramsden** (a high sheep valley and Wistan's fold) → **Honeybourne** (bees, orchards, nobody hurries) → **Blakefell** (a long bare fell with cairns and a singing wind) → **Sedgefen** (a grey fen in fog, crossed by the Stepping Way) → **Windley** (a windmill village on the open wold) → blown back within sight of Candlemere → west along the Swale → **Thornholt** (a thorny wood and Edith's geese) → **Stanbury** (the keepers' shelter and the Keepers' Stones, always in daylight) → **Oxley** (Ebba's farm) → **Wardlow** (the watch hill and the beacon). Full meanings are in the canon's gazetteer.

### The calendar

- **The year turns like old country life.** Autumn: Hild's tray at the Moot Hall door, the drove setting out, blackberries and apples. Winter: snow, the longest night, and Beacon Night. Spring: floods and the spring fair (Season 2). The long winter of Season 4 is its own story.
- **Beacon Night** is the longest night of the year. The children of Candlemere carried the flame up Wardlow on the old custom called **the Carrying**, and folk from every village climbed with one stick each (canon §1). **The Beacon Feast** follows the lighting.
- **Market day** in Candlemere is when every "something happened today" special is set, back before the lights began to go out (section 8).
- **The story counts nights to the longest night** ("six nights before the longest night"), never dates. No invented month names. A new festival is a canon change, not a drafting choice.

### Daily life

| | What's there |
|---|---|
| **Food** | Barley soup, honey-bread, apples, cheese, sausages, porridge, stone soup, apple cake, warm milk, a ham bone, carrots. Real food, plainly named. |
| **Work** | Sheep and the winter drove, the mill, the smithy, the bakehouse, geese and oxen, bees, wick-selling, knitting, carting. Children help with real jobs. |

### What magic exists

Only small folk-magic, the size of a village story:

- **Tied winds.** Aldwin the miller keeps the winds tied in knots on cords (Part 7).
- **A talking fox understood by children.** Ember, and the old fire-carriers who can understand her. Idea-jar creatures may talk to children too (section 8).
- **Fire that knows its family.** Every fire in the Westering is kin to the one fire on Wardlow (canon §1).
- **Small strange places:** a valley where honey-bread makes everyone sleepy and forgetful; a wind on Blakefell that sings "stay… rest here… sleep a while".

**What doesn't exist:** wizards, spells, wands, potions, curses, wishes granted, prophecies, chosen ones, people who fly, people turned into animals (Edith doesn't turn anyone into geese; people only say she does), and magic objects that do the work for the children. Magic never hurts anyone.

### The fire rules

These are the world's rules and the safety rules at once (canon §1 and §4, rule 18):

1. **A light that's taken will not catch. A light that's given will.** A given flame hops across by itself, from lantern to wick, when someone offers it and someone asks.
2. **A shared flame loses nothing.** Lighting another lamp from yours leaves yours no dimmer (Cicero's lamp).
3. **A thank-you makes a flame stand a little taller.**
4. **A spark struck fresh from a flint won't take** in the Westering. It sulks, smokes and goes out.
5. **One beacon only.** When it wakes, every hearth brightens where it stands. Never a relay of beacons.
6. **Children never strike, touch, feed or carry an open flame.** The lantern stays shut; only grown-ups open its little door. The flame hops across through the chimney to a wick or taper a grown-up holds close. Children carry the closed lantern and hold it up.
7. **Nobody ever blows a flame out.**
8. **The banking words** settle a fire for the night: "Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright."

### Animals

Farm animals (sheep, oxen, geese, hens, cows, a donkey, dogs, bees) and animals of the English countryside (foxes, badgers, hares, deer, otters, owls, larks, cranes, hedgehogs). **In the main story only Ember talks,** plus any idea-jar creature, and only children understand them. In a special, animals may talk to children (canon §8). There are no wolves or bears in the Westering, and no animal is ever a threat that hurts.

### Technology

Pre-industrial and hand-made: forges, watermills and windmills, carts, looms, knitting needles, glass windows, horn-windowed lanterns, clocks that tick (Honeybourne's have stopped), a tinker's clockwork toy, letters written by hand, and books that are rare and precious. No engines, no electricity, no screens, no gunpowder. **No weapons are used on anyone,** and there are no soldiers, armies or knights in the Westering (knights live only in the old tales people tell).

### Naming

Every new place or person follows the canon's naming rules (canon §2): true Old English roots, easy for a 5-year-old to say, the country-bus-stop test, no whimsy, nothing Harry Potter-like, no frightening words, no Tolkien names, and a name check before canon.

### Legends: spotted deeds, told true

A grown-up logs a real deed (by text or voice). It becomes a legend that opens a chapter (canon §8, Three legends).

- **Told true, not bigger.** The deed as it happened, with nothing added. The small voice stays small ("though his voice came out as small as a mouse"). The shaky knees stay shaky.
- **Real-world things stay real.** A legend isn't made world-true: the slide stays a slide, the lamp stays a lamp. But brands, screens, other people's names and named places are left out. Plain settings are fine ("at the big table", "at the park", "in class"). The name of a school, town, team, church or club never appears. *Must.*
- **Tagged with the strength the deed shows,** with its tag copied exactly from `strengths.md` (section 4.1), even when that isn't the week's strength. *Must.*
- **The frame is fixed:** "In the village of Candlemere they still tell of [Name the Epithet], who…" (or "In Candlemere they still tell of…"). *Must.*
- **The epithet has one of two shapes.** Either "Who" plus a past-tense verb from the deed (Clara Who Climbed, Alfie Who Spoke Up, Hugh Who Kept Going), or "of the" plus an object from the deed (Hugh of the Lantern). No adjective that describes the child: not bold, brave, kind, clever, steady, good, best or bravest. An epithet ends up as a permanent label in the Hall of Deeds and on a trading card, so it names what the child did, never what the child is. *Must.* *Flagged for Jon:* the canon's sample legend "Alfie the Bold Voice" (canon §8, Three legends, Hall of Deeds and hero cards) breaks this rule. Suggested: "Alfie Who Spoke Up". The canon text is unchanged until Jon agrees.
- **Effort and choices, never identity.** "Who spoke the truth before anyone asked" is right. "Who is the most honest boy" is wrong. *Must.*
- **Legends take turns between children.** One legend opens a chapter. A child's second waiting legend can't open a chapter while a sibling's legend is waiting; within one child, the oldest goes first. Every legend goes into the child's Hall of Deeds as soon as it's approved. *Must.*
- **A quiet nudge, for the parent only.** When one child has had no legend for two weeks, the parent gets a private note ("Spotted anything of Clara's lately?"). The children never see counts of who has had what.
- **The parent approves the exact line** (screen D2). If they edit it, the edit is safety-checked again.
- **Never tell someone else's wrong.** A deed that answered someone else's wrong can still be a legend, as long as nobody else's wrong is told: "who stood beside a child who was on their own, and said 'Stop. That's not kind.'" No other person is named or described as the wrongdoer. The checker tests that the legend doesn't name or describe another person's act. (This lets the Brave and Kind Stretch rungs in `strengths.md` become legends.) *Must.*
- **Never a legend about** a health event, or anything the child might not want told. *Must.*

## 9. Safety and bedtime rules

### The bedtime rules (canon §4, rules 13 to 21)

1. **Every scary thing turns out smaller, sadder or sillier** than it sounded: the giant, the Goose-Wife, the singing wind, the little dragon, the dark hill.
2. **The last page always lands somewhere warm,** even on cliffhanger nights. The hook is a question, not a threat. The darkest moment turns inside the same chapter.
3. **Cozy danger only:** weather, dark, cold, fog, rising water, being lost for a minute, grumpy hosts. Nothing is hurt, eaten or killed. No one is hurt worse than a scraped knee.
4. **The youngest is never alone for more than a page,** always within reach of a sibling or Ember. The same holds for an only child and for any child under 5. (For an only child under 5 on the long stretches between villages, see section 6, flagged for Jon.)
5. **Nobody enters where the children sleep, and nothing is taken at night.** No grown-up the family doesn't know is alone with the children in a way that could frighten. Every host is vouched for by the old law of the Keepers' Way or met in daylight.
6. **Fire safety:** the fire rules in section 7.
7. **Home is safe.** Grandma is never ill or in danger. The parents' absence is warm and never a worry.
8. **A laugh in every chapter,** usually Ember's.
9. **One breakfast set piece per part:** a scene children can act out the next morning.

### The fear scale

| Level | What it is | Allowed? |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Nothing tense | Yes |
| **1 · Cozy** | Weather, dark, fog, cold, a grumpy face at a door | Yes |
| **2 · Tense** | Lost for a minute, water rising, a shut gate, the flame going out | Yes, if it turns within the same page or two and the chapter ends warm (below). In the picture-book telling, only if the same picture shows comfort (a held hand, Ember's tail). |
| **3 · Too scary** | Any threat of harm, a chase, being grabbed or taken, a creature that wants to hurt, or a chapter that ends with the tension still open (below) | Never |

**What "ends warm" means, as a test.** *Must.*

1. **The last paragraph shows every child safe, together and comforted:** a held hand, Ember's tail, a roof, or sleep.
2. **The hook sentence may ask about the goal,** never about anyone's safety. "Would the flame hold till morning?" and "Who's behind the door?" pass. "Would they make it off the hill alive?" fails.
3. **In the picture-book telling, the final picture is the comfort image.**

A chapter that fails any of the three has its tension still open, which is level 3.

*For the packet:* two canon endings are tested against this. **Chapter 3** ends "The flame was only a blue bead. Would it hold till morning?", with Hugh and Alfie sheltering the lantern in the lee of the milestone and Clara asleep in Ember's tail: it passes, because the hook asks about the flame, and every child is together and sheltered. **Chapter 52** is outlined to end with the wind rising on the bare hilltop and the three of them huddled round the lantern, holding hands. It passes only if the drafted last paragraph ends on the held hands, in the lee of the fire-bowl, with Ember's tail round them, and no sentence asks whether they are safe. The draft is written that way; the canon beat is unchanged.

### The automatic safety check

**What it checks:** every season chapter at production (both tellings, every family-size, age-band, access and variant-block version), and every Tier B and Tier C output every night, text and pictures.

**How it runs.** The checker is a separate call with its own instructions, never inside the call that wrote the text. Each check below is one of two kinds:

- **Plain test (hard fail).** A count, a word list or a comparison with reviewed text. It fails on its own, with no judgment.
- **Judgment check (flag).** A second model reads the text and decides. It must write down its reason in a sentence. At production, a flag goes to the drafter and the packet; at night, a flag counts as a fail.

**What the keyword scans skip.** The chapter tag line, and any line quoted from the canon, are exempt from word-list scans. So "grabby" in a tag (`Thankful · between grabby and gushy`, `strengths.md`) never trips the threat list.

**What happens when something fails:**
1. **Nightly text** (Tier C) is rewritten and checked again, up to twice.
2. **Still failing:** the slot falls back to the reviewed default, and the parent sees a short note ("We left tonight's idea-jar scene out; here's why").
3. **Specials** that fail twice are not made. The parent sees a kind note.
4. **Season chapters** that fail go back to the drafter, and anything unresolved is listed in the review packet.

| **Fear level** | Threat words: teeth, claws, blood, scream, chase. The "ends warm" test's hook and final-picture parts. | Level 3 anywhere; "hungry", "grab" and "coming closer" in a threatening sense (a hungry fox wanting sausages is fine); the last paragraph's comfort. | "The fog was thick as Grandma's porridge, and somewhere in it something sniffled." | "Something in the fog was hungry, and it was coming closer." |

| **Fire safety** | "blow out", "birthday candle", a flint struck by a child | A child touching, striking, feeding or carrying an open flame, or opening the lantern | "Cuthbert held his taper to the chimney, and the flame hopped across." | "Hugh opened the little door and lit the stick himself." |
| **Strangers and secrets** | "don't tell" said to a child; a gift offered to a child to come away | Canon rule 17: a grown-up the family doesn't know alone with the children *in a way that could frighten*; anyone entering where the children sleep; Ember or anyone asking a child to hide something from a grown-up. **Allowed:** a named host on the Keepers' Way, met at the door with the siblings present, under the old law of guests (Cuthbert, Dame Blythe, Edith, Ebba), and Wistan's fold as the canon tells it | "Cuthbert opened the door, and all three of them stepped in together, out of the cold." | "A kind stranger whispered, 'Come with me, and don't tell anyone.'" |
| **Water** | Swimming; anyone under water | The water rule: a ford or stream only below the boot top, holding a hand, never where the water is rising or running fast; rising water solved any way but going up and waiting | "They climbed the old alder and held on until the water sank." | "Hugh jumped into the fen to fetch the tin." |
| **Personal data** | Surnames, dates, and names not in settings; the family's town and school if entered anywhere | Health, family troubles, and other real details (section 8) | "…who said his idea out loud at the big table." | "…who told Mrs. Green at Maple Street School…" |

| **Originality** | Tolkien names (canon §2 list) | Tolkien images (section 4.6); names that fail the canon's name check | "the old keepers' shelter" | "a barrow on the watch-tower hill" |
| **Slot integrity** | Any difference from the selected reviewed blocks outside a slot; a Tier C slot rendered without `seen_by_parent = true` | n/a | Only the slot differs from the reviewed blocks. | A reviewed line reworded while filling the idea jar. |
| **Length and format** | Word counts, the tag line (copied from `strengths.md`), the opening block, Pause & ask position, the last page order, the reading lines, every picture pinned to a paragraph | n/a | Within the ranges in sections 2 and 3. | A 1,400-word chapter; the tag line reworded. |
| **Pictures** | n/a | Every new picture against the scary-images list and the fear scale; the avatar matches the builder's settings | The little dragon curled on a stone, tail round its toes. | Glowing eyes in the reeds behind the children. |

## 10. The faith toggle

Faith-friendly is a toggle the family sets on the "What matters in your house" screen (`decisions.md`). It's off unless the family turns it on.

**When it's on, the family gets:**
- **A weekly verse,** on the fridge card and as the blessing line on each chapter's last page, quoted exactly from the house translation (below). Season 1's four are settled in the canon (canon §3), with two open questions about their wording:

| Strength | Verse |
|---|---|
| Brave | "Be strong and courageous. Do not be afraid." (Joshua 1:9) |
| Keep Going | "Let us not grow weary in doing good." (Galatians 6:9). *Matches no single translation* (`strengths.md`, flag 4). |
| Bounce Back | "Though the righteous fall seven times, they rise again." (Proverbs 24:16) |
| Truth-Teller | "Speak the truth each one with his neighbor." (Ephesians 4:25). *Matches no single translation* (`strengths.md`, flag 4). |

- **Scripture among the classical echoes** (section 4.4). Where the canon lists a chapter verse (Isaiah 6:8 in Chapter 2, Proverbs 12:19 in Chapter 48, John 1:5 in Chapter 53, Matthew 5:15 in Chapter 55), it takes the weekly verse's place on that chapter's last page *(flagged for Jon)*.
- **Bible stories in the library,** retold under the same bedtime rules as everything else (the "Faith" shelf and the retold shelf, canon §8).
- **A faith line in specials** where it fits (the sample: Jacob and Esau make up).

**One house translation.** *(Flagged for Jon: he picks NIV, ESV or NKJV.)*
- Every weekly verse and every fridge-card verse is quoted exactly from the one house translation, and the translation is named on every verse card ("Joshua 1:9, ESV"). *Must.*
- KJV is used only where its old wording is the point (a chapter echo such as "Here am I; send me"), and never on the fridge card, which a 3-year-old sees every day. *Must.*
- **A verse that matches no translation isn't printed** until Jon picks one. For Season 1 that is Galatians 6:9 and Ephesians 4:25. `strengths.md` flag 4 suggests NKJV Galatians 6:9 ("let us not grow weary while doing good") and ESV Ephesians 4:25 ("let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor"); once Jon picks the house translation, both are re-quoted in it. This is a decision in the Season 1 review. *Must.*
- Once the house translation is picked, every Seasons 2 to 4 verse in `strengths.md` is re-quoted in it. Several there are KJV today ("Ponder the path of thy feet", "He that hearkeneth unto counsel is wise"), which is too old for a fridge card.

**Tone.** Reverent and simple. A verse is quoted exactly, with its reference and translation, and never explained in the story. Nobody in the Westering preaches, and a verse is never used to scold ("God doesn't like fibbers" is a fail). The echo is a shape the family may notice, the same as Homer or Aesop.

**When it's off, there are no gaps.** The chapter's story text is the same with the toggle on or off. The faith layer lives only in the blessing line, the fridge card verse, the Sunday plan, the library shelf and the specials' faith line. With it off, the blessing line simply isn't there, and the library shows its non-faith pick in the same slot (canon §8: *The Enormous Turnip* instead of *The Shepherd Boy and the Giant*). Every chapter already has a non-Scripture echo that stands on its own. **No story text is written so that it only makes sense with the toggle on.**

**Later seasons.** Each season's four verses are chosen when the season is drafted and listed in the review packet for Jon.

## 11. Making a season

Claude drafts each season from this bible, `strengths.md` and the season's canon. Jon reviews it once a season (`decisions.md`), in two short sittings inside that one review: first the decisions and the outline, before any chapter is drafted, then the packet. These are the steps, in order.

### The production steps

1. **The season canon.** Using `decisions.md` and the seeds in canon §6, write the season's canon in the same shape as Season 1's: cast additions, places, the four strengths with their tags, phrases and gestures (copied from `strengths.md`), one modeled behavior per part, the 12 parts, the mystery and its clues, and the name checks. Anything new that isn't in `decisions.md` is marked for Jon.
2. **The outline.** The part engine table (Tuesday pairs, breakfast set pieces, clues, Friday questions), all 60 chapter beats with lead, echo, tag and marks, the vote table, the "remember when" schedule, the mentor's schedule, and the lead count. It also writes **each chapter's job list** (reply, trial memory, jar, "remember when", vote, pocket question, plan reminder, letter, trial announcement), taken from the Marks column and the canon's exceptions. The checker reads that list, not the weekday (section 2). The checker confirms the counts before any chapter is written.
3. **Jon's first sitting: the decisions and outline sheet.** Two pages: every flag, every new name with its name check, the tags, the part table and the verses. Jon answers it before any chapter is drafted, so a rejected name, tag or arc shape costs a line, not 60 chapters.
4. **The parts.** For each part, a one-page part sheet: the place, the hosts, the modeled behavior, the echo, the Tuesday pair, the set piece, the clue, the Friday question, the Saturday trial for all three age bands, and every child's "I can…" for the week.
5. **The chapters, in both tellings.** First the chapter-book telling (the master), then the picture-book telling made from it, with every picture pinned to a paragraph of the master (section 3, "The shared read"). Every child's line is tied to its slot. Every key act gets a version for each age band that could fill that slot.
6. **Variants.** For every chapter, as reviewed blocks:
   - the 1-, 2- and 4-child versions of the beats that change (section 6);
   - the access versions of every key act that depends on seeing, hearing, walking, climbing or speaking (wheelchair, hearing aids, low vision, signs or a talker), and of both options of every vote;
   - the pronoun blocks for a child who is she, he or they;
   - the grandparent blocks: she, he or they; one or two; Nan; and remembered;
   - the parents blocks: two parents, one parent (she, he or they), or none set, so the story never guesses a family's shape (section 6);
   - a reviewed alternate name for every named cast member (Will → Wilf, Ned → Ted, Edith → Eda).
7. **Each chapter's extras.** The Pause & ask block (with "Answers first"), the reading lines ("your line" and the big-print words), the last page in its order (the why question, what happened, the calm close, and "For the morning": missions for each age band and tell it back), Wednesday's vote copy and "remember when" copy, Monday's default grandparent reply, Friday's letter, and the reviewed default for every personal slot, each the same length band as the slot.
8. **Each week's print and plan text.** The Sunday plan (with its "In real life" line where one is needed, section 5), the fridge card and talk cards (with the week's two words), the booklet text, the fold-a-book (8 pages), and the library pairing. **The print rule:** the weekly booklet prints Tier A and Tier B text only, with each personal slot filled by its reviewed default, and prints Thursday as both vote routes ("If you chose A…" and "If you chose B…"). The nightly fold-a-book is the only print item that carries that night's approved personal parts (section 8).
9. **The checks.** Run the full safety check and the checklist on every chapter, in every version. Fix, check again, and list anything left over.
10. **Jon's second sitting: the review packet** (below).
11. **Jon's notes are worked in,** the changes are listed, and the season is locked (below).

**Where it lives.** Each season in its own folder, for example `docs/redesign/story/season-1/`, using the sample family. Jon's private copy with real names is built the same way as the mockup's (`tools/build-private.py`) and never committed.

### The review packet

Jon reviews once a season, so the packet has to make that one read count. It is one document, in this order. The total reading stays about the same as before the first sitting was split out.

| Part of the packet | What Jon does with it |
|---|---|
| **1. One-page summary** | Read. The season's promise, the arc in a paragraph, the four strengths, what's new, and what changed since the outline sheet. |
| **2. Decisions still open** | Read and answer. Anything the outline sheet didn't settle, and every clash found while drafting. |
| **3. Read in full: 14 chapters** | Read aloud if possible. Chapters 1 to 3 (the free chapters and the paywall cliffhanger), one whole middle part (five chapters), the darkest hour and the turn (Season 1: Chapters 53 to 55), the season's last chapter, plus the picture-book telling of Chapter 1 and of one Wednesday. That's about 15,000 words. |
| **4. Variant samples** | Read one part in the 1-child version, one chapter in the 4-child version, and one chapter each in a "they" block, a grandfather block and one access version. |
| **5. Skim: flagged chapters only** | A card for each chapter the checker flagged: the beat in two sentences, the lead, the tag, the echo, the Pause & ask question, the last page, the flag and how it was settled. Chapters with no flags aren't carded. |
| **6. Skim: the off-page material** | All 12 Saturday trials, the fridge cards, the missions and the "I can…" lines, the votes, and the "In real life" lines. |
| **7. The checker report** | Skim. Every flag raised in production and how it was fixed. Anything still open is at the top. |

**What "reviewed by a person" covers.** Jon reads the master text of the 14 chapters above and the samples; every other chapter and every variant is machine-checked against the master (section 8, Tier A). The product's copy says exactly that.

### Open items for the Season 1 review

These are the decisions this bible raises for Season 1. Each is also flagged where it appears. None of them changes the canon until Jon says yes.

1. **Last page order** (section 2): missions and "tell it back" move under "For the morning", after the calm close. The canon's recipe (canon §3, step 7) and LastPage screen (N6) read them before the blessing.
2. **The breakfast vote** (section 2): the parent may take Wednesday's vote at breakfast.
3. **Canon N4 wording** (section 2): "Has your voice ever gone small?" and "Can you do the lantern breath…?" are yes/no questions.
4. **Picture-book lines** (section 3): the canon's "one or two lines each" can't reach 300 words; this bible uses "one to three short sentences a picture".
5. **"Alfie the Bold Voice"** (section 8): an adjective epithet. Suggested: "Alfie Who Spoke Up".
6. **"Each hero earns a stepping stone"** (section 5): the Part 6 trial screen (W4) makes stamps a per-child reward.
7. **The house translation, Galatians 6:9 and Ephesians 4:25** (section 10; `strengths.md` flag 4).
8. **An only child under 5** (section 6): who walks the long stretches.
9. **Chapters 3 and 52 against the "ends warm" test** (section 9).
10. **"Reviewed by a person" and the FAQ** (section 8): the copy should say the master and samples are read by Jon, and every variant is machine-checked.
11. **Privacy counsel** (section 8): the child's idea recording and the retention of parent notes.
12. **The serious-event help line** (section 8): written with counsel.
13. **Voiced legends** (section 8): whether legends picked from generated options, with nothing typed, may be read in the family story voice.
14. **Access versions** (section 6): read by families who live with them before launch.
15. **The world-true table** (section 8): only the first five rows (dragon to superpower) are settled in canon §3. The rows after superpower are this bible's additions; until Jon approves them, they run as proposed.

For Season 3's outline sheet: **the library doors that open to riddles** (section 4.6).

**Jon's checklist** (ten questions, one tick each):
1. Would I happily read this to my own three?
2. Is each child's act real, and does it change what happens?
3. Does the youngest do real things, not token ones?
4. Is there a laugh in every chapter I read?
5. Does any page preach, shame, or praise who a child is instead of what they did?
6. Is anything too scary for a 3-year-old at bedtime?
7. Do the hosts, the mentor and the grandparent sound like themselves?
8. Are the classical echoes quiet enough that a child would never notice a lesson?
9. Does every new name pass the bus-stop test and sound like the Westering?
10. Is anything here I'd be uncomfortable seeing on a real family's fridge?

**How to leave notes.** Every paragraph in the packet is numbered by chapter ("Ch 14 ¶6"). Jon writes each note against a number and marks it **Must** (fix before lock), **Should** (fix unless there's a good reason, which Claude gives back) or **Idea** (for this or a later season). Notes can go in the packet document itself, as comments, or in a plain list. Claude answers every note with what changed.

### Versions and the lock

- **A season is locked** when Jon's Must notes are done and he signs off. It becomes version 1.0 (for example, "Season 1 v1.0"), with the date and a one-line list of what changed since the packet.
- **After the lock,** the nightly layer uses only the locked text. Nobody edits it by hand.
- **Fixes after the lock:** a typo or a safety fix is a small version (v1.1) and applies to every family. A story change (a line, a beat) is also a new version, but it only reaches chapters a family hasn't read yet. Anything a family has already read, a name they've heard, or a vote they've made, never changes under them.
- **A change log** sits at the top of each season folder: version, date, what changed, and why.
- **Changes to this bible** follow the same path: a note in the next review packet, and Jon's yes.

## 12. Glossary

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Season** | 12 parts, 60 chapters, one strength family. Read in order: Brave, Kind & Fair, Wise, Steady. |
| **Part** | One week: five chapters (Monday to Friday) in one place, one modeled behavior, a Saturday trial. |
| **Chapter** | One night's story. Advances by reading, never by date. |
| **Telling** | The **picture-book telling** (8 to 12 pictures) or the **chapter-book telling** (about 7 minutes). Same events, same choices. When children on different tellings listen together, the reader reads the chapter-book telling and the pictures ride along (the shared read). |
| **Shared read** | The usual night: one reader, the chapter-book telling, with each picture-book picture pinned to a paragraph and the youngest's prompt at its picture (section 3). |
| **Your line** | The 1 to 3 sentences in each chapter-book chapter that a reader in the eldest slot can read aloud themselves (section 3). |
| **Big-print words** | The two or three words or short refrain in each chapter a Kindergartner can read (section 3). |
| **Variant block** | A reviewed piece of text for one setting (a pronoun, a grandparent setup, an access version, a family size). The nightly layer picks blocks; it never writes them (section 6). |
| **Slot** | A place in the text a family fills: a child, the grandparent, a legend, the idea jar, the reply. |
| **Eldest, middle, youngest** | The three hero slots the canon is written for. Families of 1, 2 or 4 bend them (section 6). |
| **Lead** | The child whose act carries a chapter. Leads rotate. |
| **Turn child** | The child whose week it is: makes the if-then plan, hears Ember's Thursday reminder, and breaks vote ties. |
| **Key act** | The deed that changes what happens, drafted for each age band (3 to 4, 5 to 6, 7 to 9). |
| **Tier A, B, C** | Season text (Jon reviews the master and samples; every variant is machine-checked); mechanical swaps from reviewed blocks; new personal text the parent sees in full first (section 8). |
| **Tag** | `Strength · between too-little and too-much`, under every chapter. Copied exactly from `strengths.md` (section 4.1). |
| **Modeled behavior** | The one act per part a child can copy tomorrow ("First the job, then the fun"). |
| **Phrase and gesture** | Each strength's spoken line and body move ("Lantern up!" with an invisible lantern held high). |
| **Pause & ask** | The why-question in the middle of every chapter, for the parent to ask. It names who answers first. |
| **Pocket question** | The question Hild gives each child to ask when she isn't there. |
| **Classical echo** | The old story a chapter quietly retells: a shape, an image or a line. |
| **The Lantern Loop** | Meet it, name it, ask why, plan, get spotted, remember when. |
| **If-then plan** | "If [a moment the child can notice], then I [one thing they can do]." Built Tuesday; on Thursday Ember says the "if" half and the child says the rest. Defaults come from `strengths.md`. |
| **Last page** | A why question (now or at breakfast), what happened, the blessing (faith on), the calm close, next time, and "For the morning": missions and tell it back (section 2). |
| **Calm close** | The last line read aloud every night: "Fire banked. Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright." |
| **The vote** | Wednesday's choice of path. Both options are safe and good; only Thursday's route changes. |
| **Idea jar** | Where children drop story ideas. Each becomes a world-true side scene, credited "This part was [Name]'s idea." |
| **World-true** | Made to belong in the Westering (a robot becomes a tinker's clockwork toy). |
| **Spotted** | A real deed a grown-up saw and logged. |
| **Legend** | A spotted deed told true, not bigger, that opens a chapter: "In the village of Candlemere they still tell of…". Its epithet names the deed (Clara Who Climbed, Hugh of the Lantern), never the child. Kept in the child's Hall of Deeds. |
| **House translation** | The one Bible translation used for every weekly verse and fridge card, named on each card (section 10). |
| **Remember when** | Ember's whispered callback that hands a child back their own brave moment, weeks later. |
| **Special** | A "something happened today" story: a real moment turned into a Candlemere market-day adventure where the kids help make it right. |
| **Saturday trial** | One real-world challenge a week, about 20 minutes, pitched to each child. The family stamps the map together; trying counts, and a played trial counts. |
| **Breakfast set piece** | One scene per part the children can act out the next morning. |
| **Rest night** | A night with no chapter. A moon shows on the lantern string; nothing is lost. |
| **The Carrying** | The old custom: the children of Candlemere carried the living flame to Wardlow each Beacon Night, guided by a fox. |
| **The Keepers' Way** | The old road to Wardlow, with a carved lantern on every seventh milestone. |
| **The Keepers' Lantern** | The old brass lantern with horn windows. Only grown-ups open its little door. Keepers' names are scratched on its base. |
| **The Keepers' Stones** | The carved stones in the shelter wall at Stanbury, each telling how a keeper fell and got up again. |
| **Beacon Night** | The longest night of the year, when the one beacon on Wardlow is fed and lit. |
| **Banking words** | "Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright." Said when a fire is settled for the night. |
| **Lantern breath** | Breathe in slowly through your nose; breathe out so gently that a flame would only lean. |
| **The flame hops across** | How a given flame passes by itself through the lantern's chimney to a wick a grown-up holds. |
| **The drove** | The winter walk taking the flocks to market, which is why the parents are away. |
| **Friday letter, Monday reply** | The children's letter home by fox each Friday, and the grandparent's answer each Monday. |
| **"Not mine to tell"** | Leaving someone else's story for them to tell: the Truth-Teller mean between fibbing and blurting. |
| **The checker** | The automatic safety and format check on every chapter, every personal part and every new picture, run as its own call (section 9). |
| **Outline sheet, review packet, lock** | Jon's two sittings each season: the two-page decisions and outline sheet before drafting, then the packet (section 11); and his sign-off that fixes the text as version 1.0. |
