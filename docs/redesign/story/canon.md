# Grit & Grace: Story canon

## Season 1: The Last Light of Candlemere

*This is the canon that the story bible (`story/bible.md`) and the Season 1 arc build on. It uses the sample family: Hugh (7), Alfie (5) and Clara (3). Each real family's kids fill the same slots: eldest, middle and youngest. `decisions.md` overrides anything here.*

---

## 1. CAST

### The three heroes

They are ordinary children of Candlemere. Nobody chose them. No prophecy names them, and no lantern "only lights for" them. They go because someone has to, and the story works because of what they choose to do. With fewer or more children, the lead rotates through whoever is there.

**Hugh, 7, the eldest** (holds up the lantern; green hooded cloak; messy auburn curls)
- **Personality:** Earnest, steady and proud to be the eldest. He loves being trusted with a real job, and Ember brings out a silly streak nobody else sees.
- **Good at:** Reading hard words (old signs, milestones, the names on the lantern). He reads at a 2nd-grade level and wants harder words. Also plans, maps, carrying the lantern steady and remembering the rules.
- **Real struggle:** He hates being wrong or slow. When a thing goes wrong he gets cross, rushes, bosses the little ones, or wants to hide the mistake.
- **The four strengths at 7:**
  - *Brave:* doing the right thing while his knees shake. He speaks politely to the giant instead of shoving the gate, and he goes back with a light.
  - *Keep Going:* breaking a big thing into small parts ("just to the next cairn"), and sticking with the hard old letters.
  - *Bounce Back:* after his own mistake (the three knots), he doesn't fall apart and doesn't shrug it off. He says "I did that" and turns around.
  - *Truth-Teller:* saying the hard true thing first ("I untied them"). Standing with his sister instead of telling on her. Telling the tale not bigger and not smaller.

**Alfie, 5, the middle one** (round glasses; blond; small backpack)
- **Personality:** Curious and funny. He counts everything, asks "how does it work?", and fills his backpack with odd treasures.
- **Good at:** Noticing small things. Counting (for Ember, who can't). Clever fixes: pebbles in the crack, the sheep's backs, hoofprints in the snow. He is a Kindergartner who likes a challenge.
- **Real struggle:** Shy with grown-ups he doesn't know. He goes quiet, or a nervous giggle bursts out at the wrong moment. When a thing doesn't work the first time, he says "I can't!"
- **The four strengths at 5:**
  - *Brave:* speaking up to grown-ups he doesn't know, wobbly voice and all (Ashcombe).
  - *Keep Going:* trying a new way instead of the same way again (pebble by pebble), and counting all hundred stones.
  - *Bounce Back:* after a bad start, "Sorry. Can we start again?" (Thornholt).
  - *Truth-Teller:* telling what really happened, his own part too (the giggle). Saying true feelings out loud: "I'm scared of the dark hill."

**Clara, 3, the youngest** (curly blond hair; red bow; cream pinafore)
- **Personality:** Sunny, bold and stubborn. She says "Me do it!" to every job, sings, and talks to animals as if they were people.
- **Good at:** Noticing how others feel. Animals trust her. Small hands for small jobs. Holding hands. Remembering home.
- **Real struggle:** "Not me!" when something spills. Melts down when she's tired. Hides behind legs with strangers.
- **The four strengths at 3** (always real acts that change what happens, never token ones):
  - *Brave:* speaking first to the cold fox and asking Grandma to let her in. Knocking on a stranger's door while holding Hugh's hand. Whispering an old ewe calm and holding on under her for six sheep-steps, with Hugh right beside her.
  - *Keep Going:* walking ten more steps herself before asking to be carried. Remembering home when everyone else forgets.
  - *Bounce Back:* homesick tears, then a lantern breath with Hugh, then "three things I'll tell Grandma," then wrapping up a soggy Ember herself.
  - *Truth-Teller:* hand on heart, "I did it," about the open gate.

### Ember the fox

- **Pronoun:** she.
- **Who she is:** A wild red fox of the fox-ways. Foxes of her family have guided every child who carried fire to Wardlow. Grandma Ruth was guided by Ember's grandmother, and she tells Ember, "You've got your grandmother's tail." (Ember: "Everyone says that.") The name comes from Old English *æmerge*, hot ashes or embers, the color of her coat. Ember saw the beacon go dark, and she won't say who was there. "That's not mine to tell."
- **Does Ember talk?** Yes, but only children understand her, plus the old folk who once carried fire up Wardlow: Grandma Ruth, Hild and Edith. Every other grown-up hears fox yips. This gives the children a secret, keeps the grown-ups ordinary, and is a fair clue in the season's mystery.
- **Voice and manner:** Quick, polite and a little grand. Her first words ever are "Good evening. I'm so sorry to bother you." She is vain about "my magnificent tail", greedy for sausages and cheese rinds, and hates wet paws. She calls the children "my lot."
- **Running jokes** (a laugh in every chapter is usually hers):
  - She can't count past four: "One, two, three, four, lots." So Alfie counts for her.
  - She can't fib. If she tries, her ears go flat and she sneezes, and everyone knows.
  - She is brave about most things and a scaredy about thunder, loud bangs and deep water, so the children sometimes get to be brave *for* her.
  - She sleeps curled round Clara like a warm red scarf.
- **What she can do:** Always find the way home, from anywhere, by nose and by stars. Remember everything anyone ever said or did. Hear and smell far off. Squeeze through small gaps. Carry the Friday letter home by the fox-ways and be back by dawn.
- **What she can't do:** Carry the lantern (fur and fire don't mix). Open latches, untie knots or read. Fight, lie or do magic. Count past four. Choose for the children. When they ask her what to do, she says: **"I know the way home. The way on is yours."**
- **Her gift and the "remember when" device:** because Ember remembers everything, she is how a strength comes back weeks later. "Remember when…?" she whispers, and hands a child back their own brave moment, from the story or from a real legend in the family's Hall of Deeds. She hands over the memory, never the answer.
- **Her rule:** Ember is the sidekick, never the hero. The humans solve every problem.
- **The family pet:** Ember is in every family's story, as `decisions.md` settles. A family can add its own pet on the Sidekick screen as a second companion. The pet keeps Grandma company by the hearth (Chapters 1 to 3 and 57 to 60), or trots alongside on the road. It doesn't talk, find the way, carry letters or bring back a "remember when": those are Ember's jobs. *A full pet swap would be a change to `decisions.md` (flagged for Jon), and these lore lines would have to change: "You've got your grandmother's tail," the fox guides of the Carrying, the fox-ways letter, and the clue that only the old fire-carriers can understand Ember.*

### The mentor: old Hild the wick-seller

- **Name:** Hild, from Old English *hild*. It is the name of St Hild of Whitby, the abbess who (Bede tells us) helped a shy cowherd, Cædmon, find his own words. One syllable, easy for a 3-year-old.
- **Who she is:** An old woman in a brown shawl with a pedlar's tray of needles, thread and lamp-wicks, and a plain walking stick. She leads a brown donkey called Dun (Old English *dunn*, dull brown) on foot. Every autumn she sets out her tray by the Moot Hall door. The children know her as the wick-woman, and Grandma has known her for years. She is not a wizard, not a guide, and not a recruiter. She sells wicks and asks one question.
- **Her secret (the season's mystery):** She was the last keeper of Wardlow Beacon. For sixty years she walked fire up the hill each Beacon Night. Fewer folk came each year. Last winter nobody came at all, her knees gave out halfway up, she had to turn back, and the beacon burned to ash on her watch. She doesn't carry the flame now because her knees can't climb the fells, and because she has learned that a beacon kept by one person goes out. She reveals all this in Chapter 45.
- **How she teaches:** Only questions, never lectures. She answers a question with a question. She waits and lets the silence work. After the children act, she asks why they did it. She never states the moral. If a child asks "What should we do?", she says, "What do you think?" The chapter's Pause & ask is built on her question, so the parent reading aloud copies her without trying.
- **Three sample questions:**
  1. "How big must a person be to carry a lantern?"
  2. "When you give some of your light away, what does your lantern lose?"
  3. "The wind wasn't yours to choose. What was?"
  - (Also: "How do you climb a hill that's too big?")
- **Pocket questions:** In Chapter 2 she gives each child one question "for your pocket," to ask when she isn't there:
  - Hugh: *"What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?"*
  - Alfie: *"What else could we try?"*
  - Clara: *"Who needs help?"*

  **The question rule:** in the story, Hild or a child asks the question out loud. The Pause & ask to the listener is a why-question built on it. In every chapter Hild misses, a child asks their own pocket question. Chapter 1 is the exception: the pocket questions start in Chapter 2. By Part 10 the children ask each other Hild's questions. That is the Lantern Loop working: the questions move from the mentor into the child.
- **When she appears:** Chapters 2, 10, 21, 35, 45, 55 and 60. She comes on Mondays, Tuesdays or Fridays, near the start of a part or after the danger. **She never appears on a Thursday** (the test night belongs to the children), and never in the middle of a crisis. She is absent for all of Parts 3, 4, 6, 8 and 10.
- **She also gives Clara a little brass bell** from her tray in Chapter 2: "Ring it if you ever lose them." It teaches a real skill (stand still, ring, call your grown-up's name) that the children use in the fog on Sedgefen.

### Grandma Ruth: the keeper of the last hearth

In the app, Grandma Ruth is the real grandparent. She reads along, records chapters and gets the share messages. In the story she is the **grandparent slot**, and every family fills it with their own grandparent: name, drawn likeness, and what the children call them.

- **Her role is Penelope:** she keeps faith at home. Her hearth is the last light in Candlemere, because every night she banks it the old way, with the old words her own grandmother used: **"Sleep low, stay warm, wake bright."** She lights the lantern from her hearth in Chapter 3. As a girl she carried fire to Wardlow herself, and her name is scratched on the lantern's base. That is why she can understand Ember, and why she lets the children go. She knows Hild's secret too, and keeps it. When a child asks whose name is worn smooth, she says, "That's not mine to tell." Leaving someone else's story for them to tell is the Truth-Teller mean: not fibbing, not blurting.
- **The scarf:** the night they leave, she casts on a scarf and knits one row for every night they are away. At the homecoming it is as long as the lane. It is her quiet Penelope weaving, and it matches the product: one lantern lit per night read.
- **The Friday letter and Monday reply:** each Friday the children send a letter home with Ember by the fox-ways. Each Monday opens with Grandma's reply. If the real grandparent left a voice note or a read-along reaction that week, the reply uses her real words. Otherwise it is one short, warm line ("Keep your feet dry and your hearts brave. The fire's banked. Love, Grandma"). Story-world facts in this canon are fine. The story never invents real-life facts about the grandparent (health, job, home, family history) beyond what the family has entered or said in a voice note.
- **When she appears in person:** Chapters 1 to 3 (home, the lane, the farewell). Chapter 34 as a light in her window, seen from far off. Chapters 57 to 60 (the homecoming, her secret question, the feast). In between she lives in what the children carry: her banking words, her lantern breath, her hearth song and the honey-bread Clara saves for her.
- **Rules:** She is never ill, never in danger and never sad for long. She never scolds and is never the mentor. The stakes are about her fire, never about her.
- **Other family setups:**
  - *No grandparent set:* the slot becomes "Nan," the old neighbor who has always minded the children. She also carried fire as a girl, and she knits the scarf.
  - *A grandparent who has died:* the family can mark them "remembered." Nan, the old neighbor, keeps the hearth, and the banking words become "what Grandpa always said." The parents are still away on the drove, the same as in every family.
  - *Two grandparents:* both can stand at the hearth.
- **The children's parents** are away over the fells on the winter drove, taking the flocks to market, and are due "home by the longest night." That is why Grandma is minding the children. Chapter 1 has their warm goodbye note on the mantel. They are never a source of worry in any chapter, and they are home for the feast in Chapter 60.
- **Why the grown-ups let the children go:** it has always been a children's walk. On the old custom called **the Carrying**, the children of Candlemere carried the living flame from the oldest hearth along the Keepers' Way to Wardlow, guided by a fox, and every village sent grown-ups up the hill with one stick each. By old law any child carrying the Keepers' Lantern is fed and housed at any door on the Keepers' Way. Grandma did it at seven. "Nobody's too small to carry a light," she says. "But nobody carries it alone."

### What is dimming the lights of Candlemere

**For a 3-year-old:** "All the fires in the Westering are one family, and the beacon on Wardlow is their grandmother. When she went out, they all grew tired and sad, and one by one they began to fall asleep."

**The world's rule.** Long ago the first folk to come into the Westering lit a great fire on Wardlow so the families behind them could find the way. Every hearth in the land was first lit from it and carried home in lanterns. So every fire in the Westering is kin to that one fire. A spark struck fresh from a flint won't take in the Westering. It sulks, smokes and goes out. A flame only lives if it is passed on. Three small rules follow, and the whole season rests on them:
- **"A light that's taken will not catch. A light that's given will."** (Carved on the biggest of the Keepers' Stones.) A given flame leaps across by itself, from lantern to wick, when someone offers it and someone asks.
- **A shared flame loses nothing.** Lighting another lamp from yours leaves yours no dimmer (Cicero's lamp).
- **A thank-you makes a flame stand a little taller.** Gratitude feeds it.

Every year on **Beacon Night**, the longest night of the year, the children of Candlemere carried the flame up Wardlow, and folk from every village climbed with one stick each to feed the beacon. A beacon fed by one person goes out. It needs everyone's stick.

**Why it went out.** Nobody did it, and everybody did it a little. Year by year fewer folk made the walk. "It's a long way." "Someone else will go." The Carrying was forgotten. Last winter nobody came but old Hild, and her knees gave out halfway up. With no wood and no one to feed it, the beacon burned to ash on her watch. Since then the fires of the Westering have been growing tired. Lamps burn low and go *pfft*. People go yawny and grey and say "What's the use?" and shut their doors. It is never scarier than that: a hush, a yawn, a dim window. Candlemere lasted longest because Grandma never once let her hearth go out.

**The stakes.** If the beacon isn't lit by the next longest night, Grandma's fire, the last one, will fall asleep too, and the whole Westering will be dark and cold all winter. That is serious, but never frightening. Nobody is hunted, hurt or lost for good.

**Why relighting it fixes it.** Carry the last living flame back to Wardlow and give it, and the beacon wakes. Once it burns, every tired hearth in the Westering brightens where it stands, and everyone who climbed carries new fire home. The flame's journey is an Odyssey too: it is going home.

**No villain.** The nearest thing to an antagonist is Wistan, a bad host who is shown mercy and repays it. The real trouble is a forgetting that the whole land shares, and the whole land mends it by coming up the hill. Hild owns her part, Reeve Osric owns his, and the children honor Hild anyway: she lays the last stick. Mercy is always possible, because nobody in the Westering is evil. They are only tired, scared, lonely or forgetful.

## 2. THE WORLD

**Candlemere** is a village of stone cottages and slate roofs round a still, round mere. On a calm night the mere holds every lit window upside down, like a second village underwater. That is the name: *candle-mere*. It has a Moot Hall with a lamp over the door, a mill on the stream, a bakehouse, a green, and Grandma Ruth's cottage with its big inglenook. **The Westering** is the land around it, rolling west toward the sunset: hedgerows and holloways, fells and fens, old roads marked with milestones. **Wardlow**, "the watch hill," rises at its far western edge. On its bare green top stands **the beacon**: a great stone fire-bowl on a low stone step, older than anyone remembers.

**How it feels:** grounded, old and cozy. Hearths and inglenooks. Hedgerows full of blackberries. Mill streams and stepping stones. Wet fells with sheep on them. Fens full of reeds and fog. Old roads with a carved lantern on every seventh milestone, so the keepers could find their way. The magic is small and folk-sized: winds tied in knots, a fox who talks to children, fire that knows its family. Food is real: barley soup, honey-bread, apples, cheese, sausages. People are ordinary, kind or grumpy or scared, and they can change. Every night ends somewhere warm. It should feel like a place where your great-grandmother grew up.

**The Keepers' Lantern.** Old brass, horn windows on four sides, a ring handle, a little door that only grown-ups open, and a small brass chimney on top. A given flame hops in and out through the chimney. Scratched on the base are the names of everyone who ever carried fire to Wardlow in it: Grandma's among them, and one name worn almost smooth (Hild's). In Chapter 60 the children's names are added. It is the brand lantern: one lit for every night read.

### Naming rules for future seasons

**Useful endings:**
- *-mere* (lake), *-combe* (small valley), *-den* (valley or pasture), *-wold* (high open land), *-holt* (wood), *-hurst* (wooded hill)
- *-ford* (river crossing), *-bourne* (stream), *-brook*, *-fen* (marsh), *-fell* (bare hill), *-low* (hill or mound; check it doesn't suggest a barrow), *-don* (*dūn*, hill; prefer it for new hill names)
- *-ley* (clearing or meadow), *-ton* (farm or village), *-stead* (place or farmstead), *-worth* (enclosure), *-bury* (old fort), *-stow* (meeting place), *-well* (spring)

**Useful first parts:**
- Trees and plants: *ash*, *oak*, *thorn*, *hazel*, *alder*, *beech* (Old English *bōc*, which also meant "book"), *sedge*, *rush*
- Animals: *ox*, *ram*, *lark*, *crane*, *brock* (badger), *fox*, *hart* (deer)
- Other: *ald*/*eald* (old), *stan* (stone), *ward* (watch), *honey*, *candle*, *cold*, *blake* (dark), *wind*, *east*/*west*, *up*/*nether* (upper and lower), *twy* (double)

**Rules:**
- A 5-year-old must be able to say it on the first try. Two parts, three syllables at most. Spell old letters the modern way (*th*, never *þ*; *ash*, never *æsc*).
- It must sound like a real English village. The test: could it be a stop on a country bus route?
- Every name has a true root and a plain meaning. We don't invent roots.
- No two names in one season that look or sound alike (no "Wardley" next to "Wardlow").
- **One beacon only.** Never a relay or chain of beacons lit hill to hill. When the beacon wakes, each hearth brightens where it stands.
- **Check every new name** against a Tolkien name index and a real-place search before it goes into canon. Every *-bourne* and *-fell* name gets the Tolkien index check before it goes in, because the kit's own parts can build Tolkien names (*up* + *-bourne* = Upbourn; *cold* + *-fell* = Coldfells).
- **Avoid:**
  - Whimsical or cute compounds: Sparkledale, Moonwhistle, Puddlebottom, Whispering-, Twinkle-, Dragon's-.
  - Anything Harry Potter-like: Godric-, Hog-, "Little Whinging" jokes, Latin-ish spell words.
  - Frightening words: Dread-, Doom-, Grim-, Skull-, Death-.
  - Tolkien names and near-copies: Bree, Bywater, Hobbiton, Buckland, Michel Delving, Rivendell, Weathertop, Withywindle, Brandywine, Edoras, Aldburg, Dunharrow, Harrowdale, Westfold, Eastfold, Westmarch, Westernesse, Upbourn(e), Snowbourn(e), Coldfell(s), Deeping, Waymeet, Stock, Staddle, Archet, Combe (standing alone), Crickhollow, Frogmorton, Overhill, the Shire (or any "-shire" or "by-Water" name), Entmoot, "Gaffer", "hobbit", "barrow".
  - Tolkien images: no watch-tower on a hill, no burial mounds or barrows, no lights in marsh water, no grasping old willow, no riddle game in the dark, no beacons lit hill to hill, no "the road goes ever on" phrasing.
  - Famous real places (Oxford, Scafell, Canterbury), and "-land" or "-realm" names.
  - People's names that clash with a screen name in the app.
- **Bridge rule:** no bridge is a Season 1 set piece or title. Season 1 crosses water only by ford, stepping stones and the river road. The Broken Bridge belongs to Season 2.

**Name check (done from memory; run a formal check before canon is locked):**
- *Real English places we share a name with:* Wardlow (Derbyshire), Ashcombe (Devon), Ramsden (Essex, Oxfordshire), Honeybourne (Worcestershire), Windley (Derbyshire), Oxley (Staffordshire), Stanbury (West Yorkshire), Blake Fell (Cumbria), and the River Swale (North Yorkshire; also the Swale channel in Kent). "The Swale" replaces the old river name "Wend," which sounded like "wind" read aloud. All are small and fit the bus-stop test. Keep them only if Jon is happy with real names.
- *Tolkien:* "Aldbury" was dropped because it sits too close to Aldburg in Rohan; Stanbury replaces it. "Hild" is a real saint's name, but it also appears once in Tolkien's appendices (Helm's sister). Keep it unless Jon prefers a swap; the fallback is **Wynn** (Old English *wynn*, joy). Wardlow is a "watch hill", never a watch-tower.

## 3. SEASON SHAPE

- **Season 1:** 12 parts, one a week, of 5 nightly chapters (Monday to Friday): **60 chapters.**
- **Chapter-book telling:** about 7 minutes read aloud, about 900 to 1,100 words, over about 6 pages.
- **Picture-book telling** (for little ones): about 300 to 450 words over 8 to 12 pictures, one or two lines each. Same events, same choices, same Pause & ask, and it always keeps the youngest child's act.
- **Strengths:** Parts 1–3 **Brave**. Parts 4–6 **Keep Going**. Parts 7–9 **Bounce Back**. Parts 10–12 **Truth-Teller**. All four belong to the Brave family (Courage), so the whole season is "Season 1 · Brave."

### The four strengths

| Strength | Golden mean, kid words | Golden mean, grown-up words | Phrase + gesture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brave** | Between **timid** (hiding from what's right because you're scared) and **reckless** (rushing in without thinking). | Courage, between cowardice and rashness (Aristotle). Fear is felt, then mastered for a good reason. | **"Lantern up!"** Hold an invisible lantern high and take one step forward. It means "I can do it scared." |
| **Keep Going** | Between **giving up** (quitting at the first wobble) and **stubborn** (doing the same thing over and over, never resting, never asking for help). | Perseverance, between softness (giving in to discomfort) and obstinacy (rigid persistence). | **"Not yet… keep going!"** Hands climb an invisible ladder. |

### How every chapter teaches (the recipe)

1. **Opening:** "Last time…" in one line, whatever the gap. Any approved legend can open the chapter ("In the village of Candlemere they still tell of…"). On Mondays, Saturday's trial comes in as the story's memory, and Grandma's reply is read.
2. **The problem arrives in the first minute.**
3. **The mean made visible:** someone does too little and someone does too much. It is gentle and often funny. Nobody is mocked, and the too-far characters are helped or shown changing. (This is Tuesday's main job; see the part table.)
4. **Pause & ask** in the middle. In the story, Hild asks her question out loud, or a child asks their pocket question when she's absent. The Pause & ask to the listener is a why-question built on it. (Chapter 1 is the exception: the pocket questions start in Chapter 2.)
5. **The modeled behavior,** done concretely by a child, with the phrase and the gesture.
6. **The reward shown:** trust, honor, friendship, a welcome at the door. No prizes.
7. **Close:** one "Why do you think…?" question, tomorrow's mission for each child, a "tell it back" idea, and a hook that is a question, not a threat.

**Banned:** "And so they learned…", or any narrator moral. The last page may name what happened ("Hugh was scared, and he went anyway"), never what the child should learn. **Praise effort and choices** ("You tried a new way!"), never identity ("brave girl," "good boy").

## 4. THE SEASON 1 ARC: "The Last Light of Candlemere"

**The shape.** It is the Odyssey's shape: out into the world and home again. The lights of Candlemere are going out. A polite fox knocks at the window, and when every grown-up along the lane says "Not I," three ordinary children say "I'll help." They carry the last flame west along the Keepers' Way. They meet good hosts and bad hosts, and at every dark village they share the flame and ask for one stick of wood on the longest night. They get past a lonely giant by being polite and clever instead of strong, and then show him mercy. They almost forget home in a honey-sweet valley. They climb Blakefell, where the wind sings "stay and sleep," and hold on through the fog on Sedgefen. Then Hugh's one careless moment with the knotted winds blows them all the way back, close enough to see Grandma's window, and they choose to turn around. They follow a trail of small clues to the old keepers' stones and learn the truth about the last keeper. They tell the truth when it's hard. At the top of Wardlow, on the longest night, comes **the darkest hour**: the fire-bowl is empty, and the last flame goes out. They don't pretend. They say how they feel and hold hands. Then comes **the sudden joyful turn**: one light is climbing the hill below, then ten, then a hundred. Everyone they helped is coming, each carrying a lamp lit from theirs and one stick. The flame they gave away comes back to them. The beacon blazes, every hearth in the Westering brightens where it stands, and **the homecoming** follows. They are carried home asleep, known first by an old dog, tested by Grandma's secret question, wrapped in a scarf as long as the lane, and asked to tell their tale truly. Their names go on the lantern, under everyone who came before.

**The mystery a child can follow:** *"Who was the last keeper, and why did nobody come?"* One small clue lands in every part (listed below), each Friday ends on a question, and the answer comes in Chapter 45. After that the question becomes *"Will anyone come this time?"*, answered on Wardlow in Chapter 53.

### Rules that hold everywhere

**The classical engine**
1. **Virtue is shown rewarded** with trust, honor, friendship and help that comes back. It is never only vice punished. Nobody is humiliated for a mistake: mistakes are owned, then they're over.
2. **No preaching.** No narrator morals and no "And so they learned…". Hild asks, the children act, and the reader asks "why?" Praise effort and choices, never identity.
3. **Ordinary small folk, quiet courage.** The children are not chosen. Hospitality, loyalty, mercy and keeping your word carry the plot.
4. **Hospitality both ways.** Good hosts feed first and ask later. Good guests knock, say thank you, share their light and leave something behind. Bad hosts can change.
5. **Mercy is always possible.** No purely evil being exists in the Westering. Whoever is grumpy is lonely, scared or forgetful underneath.
6. **Gratitude to those who came before:** the names on the lantern, the Keepers' Stones, Grandma's banking words. The old ways are honored, never mocked. A thank-you makes a flame stand taller.
7. **Owning mistakes.** Every child makes at least one real mistake and owns it: Hugh the knots, Alfie the giggle, Clara the gate. The grown-ups own theirs too: Hild and Reeve Osric.
8. **The children solve the problems.** Ember remembers, Hild asks, grown-ups host and help. The choice and the deed always belong to a child. Ember: "The way on is yours."
9. **Share the lead.** Each child leads about a third of the chapters. The 3-year-old's acts are small and real, and they change what happens.
10. **Lights shared, never hoarded.** A shared flame loses nothing. A light that's taken will not catch; a light that's given will. The ending depends on this.
11. **Original.** No Tolkien names, places or plots. One beacon only, never a relay. No watch-towers, barrows or marsh-lights. The classical echoes are quiet: a shape a grown-up may notice, never a lesson about Homer.
12. **No bridges in Season 1.**

**Bedtime rules**
13. **Every scary thing turns out smaller, sadder or sillier than it sounded:** the giant, the Goose-Wife, the singing wind, the little dragon, the dark hill.
14. **The last page always lands somewhere warm,** even on cliffhanger nights. The hook is a question, not a threat. The darkest moment turns inside the same chapter.
15. **Cozy danger only:** weather, dark, cold, fog, rising water, being lost for a minute, grumpy hosts. Nothing is hurt, eaten or killed. No one is hurt worse than a scraped knee.
16. **Clara is never alone for more than a page.** She is always within reach of a sibling or Ember.
17. **Nobody enters where the children sleep, and nothing is taken at night.** No grown-up the family doesn't know is alone with the children in a way that could frighten. Every host is vouched for by the old law of the Keepers' Way or met in daylight. Wistan never touches them, and his "Guests stay" is loneliness, shown in the same chapter: in Ch 12 he brings warm milk and a blanket and mutters, "Everybody goes. Nobody stays."
18. **Fire safety.** Children never strike, touch, feed or carry an open flame. The lantern stays shut, and only grown-ups open its little door. A given flame hops across by itself through the lantern's chimney, to a wick or taper a grown-up holds close. Children carry the closed lantern and hold it up. Nobody ever blows a flame out.
19. **Home is safe.** Grandma is never ill or in danger; the stakes are about her fire, never about her. The parents' absence is warm ("home by the longest night") and never a worry.
20. **A laugh in every chapter,** usually Ember's.
21. **One breakfast set piece per part:** a scene kids can act out the next morning (see the part engine table).

## 5. "I CAN…" LADDERS

Every rung is something a parent can see. **Middle** must stretch a Kindergartner who likes a challenge. **Big** must stretch a 2nd grader who likes a challenge.

### Brave

| Level | "I can…" | What a parent sees |
|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can try something new with a grown-up close by. | She tastes a new food. She goes down the big slide. She says hi to a neighbor while holding your hand. She goes into a dim room to fetch something while you wait at the door. |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can say my idea or my question out loud, even to grown-ups I don't know. | He orders his own food at a café. He asks the librarian for a book himself. He tells the teacher when he needs help. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can do a hard right thing even when I'm scared: speak up, go first, or ask someone new to play, and tell you afterwards what scared me. | He asks a child who's alone to play. He reads his work aloud to the class. He tells a coach or teacher when something isn't fair. |
| **Stretch** | I can be brave for someone else, even when others aren't. | He sticks up for a child being teased while friends laugh. He says no to a dare and says why it's reckless, not brave. He goes first at something new so a younger sibling will follow. |

### Keep Going

| Level | "I can…" | What a parent sees |
|---|---|---|
| **Little** | I can try one more time. | She turns the puzzle piece a new way before handing it to you. She tries the shoe again after it goes on the wrong foot. |
| **Middle** | I can keep going when something is tricky, and try a new way. | He finishes a row of a tricky letter (the wobbly S) and asks for help on just that one. He rebuilds a fallen tower a different way. He sounds out a new word instead of guessing. |
| **Big** | I can break a hard thing into small steps and stick with it for days. | He practices just the tricky four bars, not the whole song. He reads a chapter book across a week. He tries two new ways before asking for help. |
| **Stretch** | I can make my own plan for a big goal and keep it up, even on days I don't feel like it. | He makes a chart for a two-week goal (learn a three-verse poem by heart, swim a width of the pool, learn a new piece on an instrument) and checks it off each day himself. |

### Bounce Back

| Level | "I can…" | What a parent sees |
|---|---|---|
| **Little** | I can say how I feel and take a lantern breath with a grown-up, then try again. | You breathe together first. The tower falls: "I'm mad," a breath, then she builds again. After a tumble, a cry and a cuddle, then back to play. |
| **Middle** | I can say "Oops, I can fix it," and fix it myself, without being told, even when I'm cross. | He rebuilds his own knocked-over tower without help. He redoes a page he scribbled on. He loses a game, says "Good game," and plays again. After a bad start with a friend, he asks "Can we start again?" |
| **Big** | I can get it wrong in front of others, calm myself without blaming anyone, and try again. | After a rough spelling test he practices those words. After losing a match he names one thing to do differently. After a quarrel he goes back and makes it right. |
| **Stretch** | I can help someone else get back up, and laugh at my own mistakes. | He helps a younger sibling rebuild after a crash. He tells the family a funny story about his own mistake. He tries again in front of the people who saw him fail. |

### Truth-Teller

| Level | "I can…" | What a parent sees |
|---|---|---|
| **Little** | I can say "I did it" when something spills or breaks. | She tells you she spilled the milk. She says "me" instead of "not me." |
| **Middle** | I can tell what really happened, my part too, even if I might get in trouble. | He tells both sides of a sibling fight, including what he did. He tells you he broke a toy before you find it. He says "I don't know" instead of making something up. |
| **Big** | I can tell the truth before anyone asks, and own my part. | He tells you about the broken lamp before you notice it. He says "I started it too." He hands back extra change at a shop. He tells what happened not bigger and not smaller. |
| **Stretch** | I can tell a hard truth kindly, and I won't go along with a fib. | He tells a friend kindly that he doesn't want to play that game. He won't join a group fib. He says "I was wrong" in the middle of an argument. He tells a teacher about a mistake nobody saw. |

## 6. SEASONS 2–4

**The seasons are read in order.** Every family starts with Season 1 · Brave, then Kind & Fair, Wise and Steady. Each season builds on the one before: Season 2 opens with the villages that climbed Wardlow and Edith's geese, Season 3 ends with the children's own book of the last light, and Season 4 keeps the promise made in Chapter 60. So there is no season picker.

**Season 2 · Kind & Fair · "The Broken Bridge."** (Kind, Fair Play, Thankful, Better Together.) Spring comes to the Westering. The villages that climbed Wardlow together remember they need each other, and now that the lights are back, everyone can see what the dark was hiding. The old stone bridge over the Swale at Twyford ("double ford") has fallen. The spring floods have drowned both old fords, and the bridge was the only dry way over. Upton says Netherton let it crumble. Netherton says Upton's carts broke it. The spring fair can't happen, and families are split across the river. Edith's geese brought the news at the end of Season 1. The children listen to both sides, share the work fairly, find the mason's mark on each old stone and match the marks to the families who still live on both banks, and get two stubborn villages working as one. Cuthbert forges the irons, Wistan carries the stones, and Edith's geese carry messages across the water. Echoes: Aesop's *Two Goats* on the narrow way and *The Four Oxen and the Lion* (four oxen are safe while they stand together, and not once they quarrel; a nod to Ebba's white oxen), the Round Table where no seat is higher, and Solomon's judgment (faith toggle). **Finale image:** both villages build out from their own banks and meet in the middle, where the children set the keystone and hang a lantern lit from the Wardlow beacon on it, while everyone cheers from both sides.

**Season 3 · Wise · "The Silent Library."** (Curious, Think It Through, Open Mind, Make Something New.) Hild learned the keepers' lore as a girl at the old library at Beechholt (Old English *bōc* meant both "beech" and "book"), where the Westering keeps its know-how: how to mend, grow, heal and build, and the true tales of the land, including why the beacon matters. The library has gone silent. Nobody asks questions there any more, so the words are fading from the pages, and the old librarian has stopped talking because no one asks her anything. Its doors open only to good questions, set as old English riddles in daylight. The children bring the pages back by wondering, thinking things through, changing their minds when they learn something new, and at the end writing something new: the true tale of the last light. Echoes: Socrates and his questions, the old English riddle-books, Solomon asking for wisdom (faith toggle). **Finale image:** the great hall murmuring again, with children reading aloud to grown-ups by lamplight, and the children's own book, *The Last Light of Candlemere*, placed on the shelf beside the oldest ones.

**Season 4 · Steady · "The Long Winter."** (Pause Button, Wait Well, One Thing, Enough.) The hardest winter anyone remembers. Snow shuts the roads, the stores must last until spring, and the beacon must be fed by turns, as the village promised in Chapter 60. The children take their first turns as beacon-walkers, with Hild riding in Ebba's cart. They learn to pause before they snap, to wait well for the thaw, to do one thing at a time through long dark days, and to see when they have enough and share the rest with snowed-in neighbors. Echoes: Joseph and the lean years (faith toggle), *The Ant and the Grasshopper* retold with mercy (the ants share), Seneca on having enough, and Penelope's patience. **Finale image:** on the first morning of the thaw, a snowdrop by Grandma's door, and on Wardlow the beacon still burning, because everyone took their turn.

**Season 2 teaser (Season 1 finale screen):**
> The geese are back with news: the old bridge over the Swale has fallen, and the villages on either side can't agree who should mend it.
