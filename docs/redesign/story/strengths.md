# Grit & Grace: The 16 strengths

*Part of the story bible. It uses the sample family: Hugh (7), Alfie (5) and Clara (3).*

This file sets out all 16 strengths, one section each, in the same format. Claude uses it to draft each season and to do the nightly personal parts, so every rule here can be checked. Jon reads it once per season.

**What wins when documents disagree.** `decisions.md` wins over everything. `story/canon.md` wins over this file. When a later season's canon is written and approved, it wins over this file for that season. Where the canon already settles something (the four Season 1 strengths, the Kind pair), this file copies it word for word and says so.

**What's settled and what's new.**
- **Settled:** the 16 names, grown-up names and kid lines (`feature-plan.md` section 4). The season order: Brave, Kind & Fair, Wise, Steady. About three weeks per strength. Everything about Brave, Keep Going, Bounce Back and Truth-Teller that the canon gives (sections 3, 5 and 8). The Kind pair.
- **Proposed here, for Jon's OK:** everything else. That is the other eleven golden-mean pairs, their phrases and gestures, their ladders, if-then plans, echoes and books, and a few extras for the Season 1 strengths (marked **Proposed** where they appear).
- **The four families follow `decisions.md`, not the classical filing.** For example, Aquinas puts truthfulness under justice (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 109), patience and perseverance under courage (q. 136–137), and humility and studiousness under temperance (q. 161, q. 166). Grown-up notes never say a strength belongs to a cardinal virtue "by tradition". They say it belongs to that family in Grit & Grace.

**Which side is which.** Each tag names the too-little side first, then the too-much side. There is one exception: the canon's Part 12 tag, `Truth-Teller · between making it bigger and making it smaller`, which follows Aristotle's order and puts boasting (the too-much side) first. So never work out a side from word order. Every strength has a **Sides** line under its tag (`too_little = …`, `too_much = …`), and the Tuesday builder, the parent's "too little / too much" labels and the checker read that line.

The Little gestures are Proposed for all 16, including the canon four. Where the canon gesture is already one move, the Little form is the same gesture, so the canon wording doesn't change.

**The Kind pair: kept as the canon has it, `Kind · between cold and pushy`.** The canon calls it a working pair and leaves the final choice to the bible (canon section 8, the special "The Last Red Feather"). The best alternative was "between walking past and taking over". It is plainer, but it's longer, and "pushy" already means taking over to a child. It isn't clearly better, so the canon's pair stays, and the special needs no change.

**Every phrase and every gesture is different.** No two of the 16 share a phrase. No two gestures use the same move: one lifts a lantern, one climbs, one springs, one rests on the heart, one presses, one opens a fist, one makes blinkers, one measures, one pats an arm, one balances, one goes from the chin out to a person, one laces fingers, one peeks, one taps the head and counts fingers, one opens a book, one draws and shapes. Only Brave lifts anything. The Little forms are different from each other too. When a later season changes any of them, check this list again.

## How to use this file

**For Jon.** Each strength has the same eleven parts, in the same order. The Season 1 strengths are copied from the canon, so the new decisions are in Seasons 2, 3 and 4. The things that need your OK are collected at the end, under "Flags for Jon".

**For Claude.** Check every chapter against these rules.

1. **Copy the tag exactly.** Every chapter carries its strength's tag, character for character: `Name · between X and Y`, with a middle dot and one space on each side. Never paraphrase it. Legends and specials carry the tag of the strength the deed shows, which need not be the week's (canon section 8).
2. **Kid words in the story, grown-up words in the notes.** The story, the fridge card and the child's cards use the kid name, kid line, phrase and gesture. The grown-up name and grown-up words appear only on parent screens and behind the "grown-up words" switch.
3. **The phrase and the gesture appear in every chapter,** spoken and done by a child at the moment of the modeled behavior (canon section 3, recipe step 5). The story describes the gesture in plain words ("Hugh held up an invisible lantern").
4. **Phrases stay short:** seven words or fewer, and a 3-year-old can say at least the first part. Where a strength gives a short form for Little, Clara uses it. A phrase names the act, never the child.
5. **Gestures stay distinct** (see the list above). New gestures use hands only, so they work sitting up in bed. The canon's Brave and Bounce Back gestures have bed versions, given in their sections as reading tips. **Every gesture has a single-move version a 3-year-old can copy** (the "Little gesture" column above). Clara and any child on the Little rung use it, in the story and on the fridge card.
6. **Tuesday shows both failings** (canon section 3, the weekly rhythm). One character does too little and one does too much, as the strength's **Sides** line says. It is gentle and often funny. Nobody is mocked. The too-far character is helped or shown changing, and a child finds the middle way. **Never give a failing a child's own catchphrase.** "Me do it!" is Clara's sunny strength in the canon, so no failing sounds like it.
7. **Modeled behaviors are concrete.** A child can do one tomorrow, at home, with nothing special, and a parent can see whether it happened. There is one per part, three per strength.
8. **Use the ladder as written.** The Sunday plan, fridge card and strength page quote the "I can…" line for the child's rung. The four rungs are **Little** (about 3–4), **Middle** (about 5–6), **Big** (about 7–9) and **Stretch** (one rung up, for a child who likes a challenge). The app picks the starting rung from age, the stretch setting moves a child up one rung, and a parent can move a child up or down. In the sample family, Clara starts on Little, Alfie on Middle (Big with stretch on), and Hugh on Big (Stretch with stretch on) (`feature-plan.md` section 4).
   **The "I can…" line comes from the rung. The examples come from the child's age.** A 5-year-old moved up to Big gets the Big "I can…" line with examples a 5- or 6-year-old can really do. A 3-year-old moved up to Middle gets the Middle line with examples for a 3- or 4-year-old. Each ladder gives these under "Moved up a rung". A 7-year-old on Stretch uses the Stretch examples as written.
   **Examples are pronoun-swapped to the child they're shown for.** In this file the examples alternate she, he and they across rungs, so no age is tied to a girl or a boy. The Season 1 ladders keep the canon's wording, and the app swaps their pronouns too.
9. **If-then plans have one shape:** "If [a moment], then I [one thing they can do]." The "if" is never a rule ("If I'm being good…"). A Middle, Big or Stretch plan may start from a feeling the child can notice ("If my voice wants to hide…"). **A Little plan is one outside moment plus one action.** Little "if" moments are things a grown-up can see and say aloud: "If my tower falls…", "If you say 'Pause!'…". A grown-up says the "if" half when it happens, and the child does the "then" half. No inner feelings ("If my tummy feels wobbly"), no wanting ("If I want more") and no questions the child must ask themselves ("ask 'Do I have enough?'"). The checker counts exactly one verb phrase after "then" in a Little plan. The only exception is the canon's Keep Going plan for Clara (see flag 13). Ember reminds the turn child of their plan in Thursday's chapter (canon section 3).
10. **Praise choices and effort, never identity.** "You asked before you helped!" Never "kind girl" or "brave boy" (canon section 3, "Banned").
11. **Echoes stay quiet.** A grown-up may notice the shape. The story never names Homer, Aesop, the Stoics or the Bible, and never teaches about them (canon rule 11). Scripture appears only with the faith toggle on. Quotations appear only in grown-up notes, the weekly verse and the strength page, never in story text.
12. **Be honest about Aristotle.** He named both failings for courage, truthfulness about oneself (*Nicomachean Ethics* IV.7), good temper (IV.5), temperance (III.10–12), generosity (IV.1) and friendliness (IV.6). Justice he calls a mean in a different sense (V.5). Only Brave and Truth-Teller's Part 12 use one of his own pairs. The other strengths borrow his words or his method (a strength between too little and too much), and the notes say the pair is ours. Never write "Aristotle said kindness is between…".
13. **Mark every quotation** as **exact** (checked wording, named translation), **canon** (the canon's wording, kept as is) or **paraphrase** (the idea, in our words). Never put a paraphrase inside quotation marks in the app. The full list is under "Quote check" at the end.
14. **Safety lines that hold for every strength, each with a checker test.**
    - **Telling a grown-up when someone is hurt, unsafe or scared is never tattling.** *Test:* any story line, mission or card that discourages a child from telling a grown-up fails. (See Truth-Teller.)
    - **"That's not mine to tell"** is about leaving someone else's story for them to tell. It never means a child keeping a secret from their grown-ups. *Test:* any line where a child keeps a secret from their grown-ups fails. (See Truth-Teller.)
    - **No strength is ever about how much a child eats, or about bodies.** *Test:* no rung, mission or story line praises a child for eating less or for tasting a food, and no child on the page eats less at a meal or goes hungry. The canon's Season 1 text is outside this test until Jon decides flag 14. (See Enough.)
    - **A "remember when" hands back a good choice or a recovery, never the mistake.** *Test:* a callback that brings back a child's mistake fails (the bible's shaming check: "a mistake raised again after it's owned"). Every "Season 1 seeds" list in this file follows this rule.

**Which strength is it?** Use this when tagging a legend or a special, or when two strengths feel alike.

| If the hard part is… | …it's this | …not this |
|---|---|---|
| doing or going, while scared | Brave | Truth-Teller (saying what's true) |
| the first step into something scary | Brave | Keep Going (the hundredth step when it's long) |
| a long or hard thing, before anything has gone wrong | Keep Going | Bounce Back (getting up after a fall or a mistake) |
| a next step you can take | Keep Going | Wait Well (nothing to do yet but let time pass) |
| carrying a goal across days | Keep Going | One Thing (holding attention for one sitting) |
| a hot feeling about to burst, in seconds | Pause Button | Think It Through (a choice with time to weigh it) |
| stopping before you act on an urge | Pause Button | Wait Well (minutes to weeks, for something not here yet) |
| saying "later" when it's time to act | Wait Well (put-it-off) | Better Together (leaving it to *someone else*) |
| giving *this* person what they need | Kind | Fair Play (everyone gets an equal turn and a say) |
| turns, shares and a say | Fair Play | Better Together (doing the work side by side) |
| seeing *who* gave it | Thankful | Enough (how much I want) |
| wanting more, or stopping at enough | Enough | Thankful |
| asking about the world | Curious | Open Mind (letting the answer change your mind) |
| giving up on an idea halfway | Make Something New | Keep Going (giving up on a hard thing) |
| saying a feeling or a fact truly ("It's fine" when it isn't) | Truth-Teller | Enough |

"I was wrong" is Truth-Teller and Open Mind together. In a Truth-Teller week, tag it Truth-Teller; in an Open Mind week, tag it Open Mind.

**Each strength section has:** 1) the name card · 2) the golden mean, tag and sides · 3) the phrase and gesture, with its Little form · 4) Tuesday pairs · 5) three modeled behaviors · 6) the "I can…" ladder with if-then plans, and examples for a child moved up a rung · 7) if-then cards for the IfThen screen · 8) classical echoes · 9) books that pair well · 10) what it gets confused with · 11) where it sits in the seasons.

### 1. Brave

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Brave |
| **Grown-up name** | Courage |
| **Kid line** | I do the right thing while my knees shake |
| **Family · season** | Brave (Courage) · Season 1, Parts 1–3 (Chapters 1–15) |
| **Status** | Canon, except the items marked Proposed |

**Golden mean** (canon section 3)

| | Too little | **Brave** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Timid:** hiding from what's right because you're scared. | Doing the right thing while you're scared. | **Reckless:** rushing in without thinking. |
| **Grown-up words** | Cowardice | Courage, between cowardice and rashness (Aristotle). Fear is felt, then mastered for a good reason. | Rashness |

**Tag:** `Brave · between timid and reckless`
**Sides:** too_little = timid · too_much = reckless
*Aristotle's own pair (Nicomachean Ethics II.2 and III.6–9).*

**Phrase and gesture** (canon)
- **Phrase:** "Lantern up!"
- **Gesture:** Hold an invisible lantern high and take one step forward. It means "I can do it scared."
- **Little gesture** (Proposed): hold the lantern high.
- *In bed:* lean forward instead of stepping. This is a reading tip only. The canon wording is unchanged.
- **Shared tool:** the lantern breath (canon section 3) belongs most to Brave and Bounce Back weeks.

**Tuesday pairs** (canon, the part engine table)
- Part 1: grown-ups along the lane saying "Not I" / Will the baker's boy runs into the dark with no light.
- Part 2: faces that duck away from the window / a big boy who dares everyone to run across the dark square and trips over the pot.
- Part 3: Alfie wants to hide in the straw / Hugh wants to shove the gate and shout.

**Three modeled behaviors** (canon section 3)
1. When something needs doing, be the first to say "I'll help." (Part 1)
2. Say hello first, and say your idea out loud, even if your voice comes out small. (Part 2)
3. When something big scares you, take a lantern breath and use your polite words: "Please may I…?" Don't shove, and don't hide. (Part 3)

**"I can…" ladder** (canon section 5) **and if-then plans** (Proposed)

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can try something new with a grown-up close by. | She tastes a new food. She goes down the big slide. She says hi to a neighbor while holding your hand. She goes into a dim room to fetch something while you wait at the door. | If we come to something new, then I say "Lantern up!" |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can say my idea or my question out loud, even to grown-ups I don't know. | He orders his own food at a café. He asks the librarian for a book himself. He tells the teacher when he needs help. | If my voice wants to hide, then I take a lantern breath and say it anyway. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can do a hard right thing even when I'm scared: speak up, go first, or ask someone new to play, and tell you afterwards what scared me. | He asks a child who's alone to play. He reads his work aloud to the class. He tells a coach or teacher when something isn't fair. | If I'm scared to do the right thing, then I ask "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?" and do the brave one. |
| **Stretch** | I can be brave for someone else, even when others aren't. | He sticks up for a child being teased while friends laugh. He says no to a dare and says why it's reckless, not brave. He goes first at something new so a younger sibling will follow. | If someone is being teased, then I stand next to them and say "Stop. That's not kind." If it isn't safe, I get a grown-up. |

The Big plan uses Hugh's pocket question from Chapter 2 (canon section 1).

**Moved up a rung** (Proposed; see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* She says hello and her name to a new child at the park, with you close by. She tells the doctor where it hurts herself.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* He asks a child sitting alone at the park to play. He shows his drawing to the class at show-and-tell. He tells the teacher when another child keeps knocking his tower down.

**If-then cards** (Proposed)
- **When…** I feel shy · it's new and scary · someone needs help and nobody's going
- **Then I…** say "Lantern up!" and take one step · take a lantern breath and say my idea · say "I'll help" first

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and folk tales** (canon, Parts 1–3): *The Little Red Hen* ("Who will help?" "Not I."). Aesop, *Belling the Cat* (everyone agrees it must be done; nobody will do it). *Stone Soup*. Aesop, *The Lion and the Mouse*.
- **Aristotle** (paraphrase): someone who runs from everything becomes a coward, and someone who fears nothing and rushes at every danger becomes rash (*Nicomachean Ethics* II.2). His full account (fearing the right things, for the right reason, in the right way, at the right time) is in Book III, chapters 6–9.
- **Homer** (canon): the Cyclops's cave (*Odyssey* 9), won by being clever and polite, not strong; they slip out under the sheep. No blinding and no lie. Shy Telemachus finds his words (*Odyssey* 3).
- **Arthurian** (canon, Part 3): Malory's knights swear to give mercy to anyone who asks (paraphrase). *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*: courtesy to the big, frightening stranger.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* David and the giant (1 Samuel 17): the smallest brother goes when the grown soldiers won't. It is the library's "The Shepherd Boy and the Giant" (canon section 8). Nobody dies on the page: the retelling ends as David steps forward.
  - *Weekly verse* (canon): "Be strong and courageous. Do not be afraid." (Joshua 1:9)
  - *Also in canon, Chapter 2:* "Here am I; send me." (Isaiah 6:8, KJV)

**Books that pair well**
- *Jabari Jumps* by Gaia Cornwall. A boy works up to the high diving board. Little and Middle.
- *Sheila Rae, the Brave* by Kevin Henkes. Sheila Rae, who fears nothing, gets lost, and her timid little sister leads her home. Both failings in one book. Little and Middle.
- *Brave Irene* by William Steig. A girl carries a dress through a snowstorm for her sick mother. Big.

*Not* The Courage of Sarah Noble: it shows Native people through a settler's fear, and canon section 8 already replaced it as the library pick.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Showing off or taking dares.** That's the reckless side. Brave needs a good reason ("the right thing"). The Stretch rung asks the child to say why a dare is reckless.
- **Not feeling scared.** Brave means scared *and* doing it. Never write a hero who "wasn't afraid at all."
- **Truth-Teller** and **Keep Going:** see "Which strength is it?" above.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 1, Parts 1–3. Candlemere (Clara lets the fox in; Hugh says "I'll help"), Ashcombe (Alfie speaks up), Ramsden (Hugh goes back to the giant's gate with a light).
- **Comes back:** in "remember when" callbacks (Ember brings back Alfie in Ashcombe in Chapter 28), in legends tagged Brave (Alfie the Bold Voice), and in every later season. Kind, in Season 2, often needs courage too.

### 2. Keep Going

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Keep Going |
| **Grown-up name** | Perseverance (grit) |
| **Kid line** | Not yet… try again, a new way |
| **Family · season** | Brave (Courage) · Season 1, Parts 4–6 (Chapters 16–30). The mockup's "Week 6". |
| **Status** | Canon, except the items marked Proposed |

**Golden mean** (canon section 3)

| | Too little | **Keep Going** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Giving up:** quitting at the first wobble. | Sticking with it, resting when you need to, and trying a new way. | **Stubborn:** doing the same thing over and over, never resting, never asking for help. |
| **Grown-up words** | Softness (giving in to discomfort) | Perseverance, between softness and obstinacy. | Obstinacy (rigid persistence) |

**Tag:** `Keep Going · between giving up and stubborn`
**Sides:** too_little = giving up · too_much = stubborn
*The pair is the canon's. The grown-up words borrow Aristotle's: "softness" (Nicomachean Ethics VII.7) and the obstinate or headstrong (VII.9). But he never presents perseverance as a mean between those two.*

**Phrase and gesture** (canon)
- **Phrase:** "Not yet… keep going!"
- **Gesture:** Hands climb an invisible ladder.
- **Little gesture** (Proposed): the same. Climbing hands are one move a 3-year-old can copy.

**Tuesday pairs** (canon)
- Part 4: the sleepers of Honeybourne / Old Alric pushing his stuck cart the same way for three days.
- Part 5: a shepherd who turned back at the first cairn / a walker who charged up and now lies flat, puffing.
- Part 6: Alfie wants to sit and wait to be fetched / Hugh wants to push on while the water rises.

**Three modeled behaviors** (canon section 3)
1. When fun pulls you away from a job, say "First the job, then the fun." (Part 4)
2. When the first way doesn't work, try a new way. (Part 5)
3. When it's long and hard, do just the next small step. (Part 6)

**"I can…" ladder** (canon section 5) **and if-then plans** (canon section 8; the Stretch plan is from `feature-plan.md` section 4)

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** | I can try one more time. | She turns the puzzle piece a new way before handing it to you. She tries the shoe again after it goes on the wrong foot. | If it's hard, then I take a lantern breath and try one more time. |
| **Middle** | I can keep going when something is tricky, and try a new way. | He finishes a row of a tricky letter (the wobbly S) and asks for help on just that one. He rebuilds a fallen tower a different way. He sounds out a new word instead of guessing. | If I want to quit, then I say "not yet" and try a new way. |
| **Big** | I can break a hard thing into small steps and stick with it for days. | He practices just the tricky four bars, not the whole song. He reads a chapter book across a week. He tries two new ways before asking for help. | If it feels too big, then I do just the next small step. |
| **Stretch** | I can make my own plan for a big goal and keep it up, even on days I don't feel like it. | He makes a chart for a two-week goal (learn a three-verse poem by heart, swim a width of the pool, learn a new piece on an instrument) and checks it off each day himself. | If it gets boring or hard, then I do one small step and check my plan. |

Clara's plan is the canon's, word for word. It has two actions and starts from a feeling ("If it's hard"), so it breaks rule 9's Little shape. It stays until Jon decides flag 13.

**Moved up a rung** (Proposed; see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* They try a new way to fit the shape into the shape-sorter. They pull their own trousers up again when they come out twisted.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* She works on a 50-piece puzzle over two days. She practices writing her name a little each day until she can do it on her own. She rides her balance bike a bit further each day.

**If-then cards** (mockup)
- **When…** it feels too big · it doesn't work the first time · I want to quit
- **Then I…** take a lantern breath and try one more time · do just the next small step · say "not yet!" and try a new way

## The Steady family (Temperance) · Season 4 · "The Long Winter"

The canon's summary (section 6): the hardest winter anyone remembers. Snow shuts the roads, the stores must last until spring, and the beacon must be fed by turns, as the village promised in Chapter 60. The children take their first turns as beacon-walkers, with Hild riding in Ebba's cart. They "learn to pause before they snap, to wait well for the thaw, to do one thing at a time through long dark days, and to see when they have enough and share the rest with snowed-in neighbors." The canon's echoes are Joseph and the lean years (faith toggle), *The Ant and the Grasshopper* retold with mercy (the ants share), Seneca on having enough, and Penelope's patience. The finale: a snowdrop by Grandma's door on the first morning of the thaw, and the beacon still burning on Wardlow.

**Everything in these four sections is Proposed,** except the names, grown-up names and kid lines (`feature-plan.md` section 4) and the Season 4 facts above.

**Proposed order,** following the list in `decisions.md`, as Season 1 does: Parts 1–3 Pause Button, Parts 4–6 Wait Well, Parts 7–9 One Thing, Parts 10–12 Enough. The season then ends on sharing the stores at the thaw.

**Aristotle and the Steady family.** Aristotle names temperance as the mean between self-indulgence and "insensibility" (*Nicomachean Ethics* III.10–12), and good temper as the mean between hot temper and a spiritless lack of anger (IV.5). Enough and Pause Button borrow his words, but the pairs are ours. His temperance covers only the bodily pleasures of touch and taste (III.10), which Enough deliberately leaves out. His too-little side of good temper is not feeling anger at all, which is not the same as bottling it up. He has no named virtue for patience or focus, so the Wait Well and One Thing pairs are ours too.

**Season 4 and food.** The canon says "the stores must last until spring" and the children "share the rest with snowed-in neighbors" (section 6). So in Season 4, **what is shared is always extra:** the spare jar, the second cheese, the full woodpile. Nobody on the page eats less at a meal, goes hungry, or is praised for eating less. The stores last because they were well kept and fairly shared, never because a child ate less.

### 5. Pause Button

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Pause Button |
| **Grown-up name** | Self-control |
| **Kid line** | Stop, breathe, then choose |
| **Family · season** | Steady (Temperance) · Season 4, Parts 1–3 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Pause Button** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Snapping:** doing the first thing the hot feeling wants: grab, push, shout, slam. | Stop, breathe, then choose. You still feel it, and you still act, after the pause. | **Bottling it up:** squashing the feeling down and going silent, never saying or choosing anything, until it bursts out later. |
| **Grown-up words** | Impulsiveness; hot temper (Aristotle's irascibility) | Self-control and good temper: feeling the emotion, then choosing the response. | Suppression (our word): feeling the anger and swallowing it. |

**Tag:** `Pause Button · between snapping and bottling it up`
**Sides:** too_little = snapping · too_much = bottling it up
*The pair is ours. It borrows from Aristotle's good temper (Nicomachean Ethics IV.5), but only the snapping side is his (hot temper). His too-little side is different from bottling it up. It's a lack of spirit: not feeling anger when you should, and putting up with insults to yourself and your own. Our bottling-it-up child does feel the anger and swallows it. So never call "bottling it up" Aristotle's failing.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "Pause… breathe… choose."
- **Gesture:** Press an invisible button in the air with one finger. Hold it down for one lantern breath. Then let go: "choose."
- **Little gesture:** press the button.
- It builds on the lantern breath from Season 1. It doesn't replace it.

**Tuesday pair** (example): a boy who throws a snowball at the window the moment he's cross (snapping) / a girl who says "I'm fine" all day with her jaw tight, then bursts into tears over a dropped mitten (bottling it up). Someone kneels beside each of them, and both get to start again.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. When you feel hot and grabby, press your pause button and take a lantern breath before you grab, push or shout. (Part 1)
2. Say the feeling in words instead of with hands: "I'm cross!" "I'm so mad it's not my turn!" (Part 2)
3. When a game gets too wild, say "Pause!", step back for three breaths, then come back and choose. (Part 3)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can stop when my grown-up says "Pause!" and take a lantern breath with them. | He freezes in a game of statues when you say "Pause!" He stops mid-grab when you say "Pause button," breathes with you, then asks for the toy. He says "I'm cross!" with a stamp instead of a push. | If you say "Pause!", then I take a lantern breath with you. |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can press my own pause button when I'm cross, and use words, not hands. | They stop before snatching a toy and say "Can I have a turn?" They walk away and breathe when their tower is knocked down, then come back. They say "I'm SO mad" instead of hitting. They wait for a gap instead of shouting over someone. | If I get cross, then I press pause, take a lantern breath, and use my words. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can notice the hot feeling starting, take a pause on my own, pick the better of two choices, and tell you afterwards what I chose. | In a sibling fight she says "I need a minute" and leaves the room, then comes back calm. She loses a game and holds back the angry word. She tells you later, "I wanted to shove him, but I went to my room instead." She doesn't answer back straight away when told no. | If I feel hot inside, then I say "I need a minute," take three lantern breaths, and choose. |
| **Stretch** | I can help someone else find their pause, and keep my own pause when everyone around me is getting wild. | He says "Lantern breath with me?" to a sibling who is melting down. He doesn't join in when the class gets silly. He stops a game that's getting too rough and suggests a calmer one. | If my brother or sister is about to melt down, then I kneel down and say "Pause button? Breathe with me." |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* She says "My turn next?" instead of grabbing, with you nearby. She stamps and says "I'm cross!" instead of pushing.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* He says "I need a minute" and sits on the stairs when a game with his brother goes wrong, then comes back. He tells you at bedtime, "I wanted to throw the block, but I didn't."

**If-then cards**
- **When…** I want to grab · I feel hot and cross · the game gets too wild
- **Then I…** press my pause button and take a lantern breath · say how I feel in words · step back, breathe three times, then choose

**Classical echoes**
- **Fairy tales:** Grimm, *Sweet Porridge*. A magic pot cooks porridge when you say "Cook, little pot" and stops when you say "Stop, little pot." One day nobody can remember the stop word, and porridge fills the whole town. It's a pause button, and it's funny. Perrault, *The Ridiculous Wishes*: a woodcutter and his wife waste three wishes on the first thing they blurt out, and a sausage ends up stuck on a nose. That's the snapping side.
- **Stoic:** Epictetus's two words to live by, "bear and forbear" (Greek *anechou kai apechou*, as Aulus Gellius reports in *Attic Nights* 17.19). Seneca, *On Anger* (*De Ira*) 2.29.1: *"Maximum remedium irae mora est."* In plain English: "The greatest remedy for anger is delay." (The Latin is exact; the English is ours.) Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5 (paraphrase): it isn't the thing that upsets us, it's what we think about it. **Aristotle** (paraphrase): anyone can get angry, which is easy; being angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, for the right reason and in the right way is not easy (*Nicomachean Ethics* II.9).
- **Homer:** in his own house, among rude guests, Odysseus feels his anger rising. He strikes his chest and tells his heart to hold still, because it has held through worse (*Odyssey* 20, near the start; paraphrase, since translations differ). He waits for the right moment instead of snapping. Tell only the pause, never what comes later in that book.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* Abigail and David (1 Samuel 25). David is angry at rude Nabal and sets off in a temper. Abigail rides out with bread and raisin cakes and asks him to stop. David stops, and thanks her for stopping him. Retell it gently: an angry man, a brave kind woman, a pause, a thank-you. **Stop at David's thank-you (1 Samuel 25:32–35).** The rest of the chapter (Nabal's death, and David marrying Abigail) is never told.
  - *Weekly verse:* "Let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger." (James 1:19, ESV) For Big readers: "Whoever is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city." (Proverbs 16:32, ESV)

**Books that pair well**
- *Llama Llama Mad at Mama* by Anna Dewdney. A shopping-trip meltdown, then a better way. Little.
- *When Sophie Gets Angry—Really, Really Angry…* by Molly Bang. Sophie runs, cries, climbs her tree, calms down and comes home. Little and Middle.
- *Sometimes I'm Bombaloo* by Rachel Vail. Katie gets so angry she uses her fists instead of words, takes some time out, and comes back. Middle.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Being quiet and good.** A child who goes silent and swallows everything is bottling it up. Pause Button always ends in *choose*, and saying "I'm cross!" is a good choice.
- **Think It Through** (Season 3). The kid lines are near twins ("Stop, breathe, then choose" and "Stop, think, then choose the wise way"). The rule: Pause Button is the brake, for a *hot feeling in the moment*. Think It Through is the steering, for a *choice with time to weigh it*. If the problem is a feeling about to burst, it's Pause Button; if it's a choice between paths, it's Think It Through. Families meet Think It Through first, so Season 4 can say "You already know how to think it through. Tonight is about the moment *before* you can think." See flag 3.
- **The lantern breath.** The breath is a tool every strength can use. Pause Button is the whole move: stop, breathe, *choose*.
- **Wait Well:** see "Which strength is it?" above.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 4, Parts 1–3. Snowed in together, tempers get short, and the beacon-walkers mustn't squabble on the hill.
- **Season 1 seeds for "remember when":** A seed used in a "remember when" is always the child's recovery or good choice, never the mistake (rule 14). Chapter 12: Hugh asks his pocket question and uses polite words ("Thank you for the shelter, sir. May we go now?") instead of shoving the gate. Chapter 38: after the door slammed, Alfie comes back the next day, knocks calmly, says sorry and asks "Can we start again?" *Alfie's nervous giggle in Chapter 37 is not a Pause Button seed. It was nerves, not a hot temper, and it's a mistake he has already owned.*
- **Before Season 4:** a parent can spot a Pause Button deed at any time and save it as a legend tagged Pause Button. Don't use the word "Steady" in Season 1–3 legend names (canon section 8).

### 6. Wait Well

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Wait Well |
| **Grown-up name** | Patience |
| **Kid line** | Good things can take a while |
| **Family · season** | Steady (Temperance) · Season 4, Parts 4–6 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Wait Well** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Hurry-up:** "Now! Now! NOW!" Whining, pestering, peeking, grabbing it before it's ready. | Waiting kindly, finding something good to do while you wait, and doing your part when it's time. | **Put-it-off:** still waiting when it's really time to act. "Later." "We'll see to it tomorrow." |
| **Grown-up words** | Impatience | Patience: waiting calmly for what can't be hurried, while still doing what can be done. | Passivity or procrastination |

**Tag:** `Wait Well · between hurry-up and put-it-off`
**Sides:** too_little = hurry-up · too_much = put-it-off
*The pair is ours. The put-it-off side already has a face in the canon: Reeve Osric's "We'll see to it tomorrow," which he says sorry for in Chapter 59.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "Seed in the snow… wait and grow." (Little: "Wait and grow.")
- **Gesture:** Make a fist: a seed under the snow. Cover it with your other hand, like snow. Slowly lift the snow hand away and let the fist open, one finger at a time, like a snowdrop coming up.
- **Little gesture:** a closed fist opening slowly.
- It fits Season 4: the snowdrop at the thaw is the season's finale image.

**Tuesday pair** (example): a neighbor who digs up his bulbs every morning to see if they've sprouted yet (hurry-up) / a carter who sits by his fire waiting for the snow to melt off his own doorstep, while the path to the woodpile stays shut (put-it-off). The children help the first plant a marker and wait, and the second pick up a shovel.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. When you have to wait, fill the wait: sing a song, count, or play I-spy. (Part 4)
2. When a grown-up is talking, put your hand gently on their arm and wait for them to turn to you, instead of interrupting. (Part 5)
3. When you want something big, wait for it and do one small thing each day to help it come: water the seed, add a coin to the jar, cross off a day. (Part 6)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can wait a little while, for a song or a count of ten, with a grown-up helping. | They wait for their turn on the swing while you count to ten. They wait for the bath to fill while you sing one song together. They put a hand on your arm while you finish a sentence. | If it's someone else's turn, then I sing a waiting song. |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can wait my turn and find something to do while I wait, without whining. | She plays I-spy in a queue instead of pulling on your sleeve. She puts her hand on your arm and waits instead of interrupting. She asks once about the park and then waits until after tidying. She plants a seed, waters it every day, and doesn't dig it up to check. | If I want to interrupt, then I put my hand on your arm and wait for you to turn. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can wait days or weeks for something good, and keep doing my part while I wait. | He saves his pocket money for several weeks for something he wants. He waits for the next chapter at bedtime without skipping ahead. He keeps a growing chart for a bean plant. He lets a friend finish talking before he answers. | If I want it right now, then I say "Wait and grow" and do one thing that helps it come. |
| **Stretch** | I can wait cheerfully for someone slower than me, and I can tell when waiting is over and it's time to act. | They walk at a little sibling's pace without complaining. They let a younger one go first. They play a waiting game to help a younger sibling wait. They see a job that needs doing and do it now instead of "later." | If I catch myself saying "later" about a job, then I ask "Is it time now?" and if it is, I start. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* He sings the whole tidy-up song while waiting for his turn with the watering can. He puts his hand on your arm and waits a moment before you turn.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* She waters a bean seed every morning for two weeks and draws it on a chart when it comes up. She waits for her birthday by crossing off the days on the calendar.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** I have to wait my turn · I want it right now · I want to interrupt
- **Then I…** sing, count or play I-spy · say "Wait and grow" and do one helpful thing · put my hand on your arm and wait

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and fairy tales:** Aesop, *The Goose That Laid the Golden Eggs*: the owner can't wait for one golden egg a day and loses them all. (Retell gently: the goose flies off, cross and unharmed, and never comes back.) Arnold Lobel, "The Garden" in *Frog and Toad Together* (a modern classic; see Books): Toad shouts at his seeds to grow, and they come up once he stops shouting and waits. *Not* Andersen's *The Ugly Duckling*: its reward is becoming beautiful, and the bible's stereotype check fails "looks tied to goodness".
- **Stoic:** "Nothing great is produced suddenly, since not even the grape or the fig is." Epictetus, *Discourses* 1.15 (George Long's translation). He goes on (paraphrase): if you tell me you want a fig, I'll answer that it takes time; let it flower first, then bear fruit, then ripen.
- **Homer** (canon: "Penelope's patience"): Penelope waits twenty years for Odysseus. To hold off the suitors she weaves a great cloth by day and quietly unpicks it at night (*Odyssey* 2, 19 and 24). Her waiting is never idle: she is keeping faith at home. Grandma Ruth's scarf in Season 1 is already this echo (canon section 1).
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* Noah and the dove (Genesis 8:6–12). The rain has stopped, but the land is still under water. Noah sends out the dove and it comes back. "And he stayed yet other seven days" (Genesis 8:10, KJV), and it comes back with an olive leaf. He waits seven more days.
  - *Weekly verse:* "To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven." (Ecclesiastes 3:1, KJV)

**Books that pair well**
- *Waiting* by Kevin Henkes. Five toys on a windowsill wait for different things. Little.
- *Waiting Is Not Easy!* by Mo Willems. Gerald has to wait for a surprise, and it's worth it. Little and Middle.
- *Frog and Toad Together* by Arnold Lobel, the story "The Garden": Toad shouts at his seeds to grow, then learns to wait. Middle and Big.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Put-it-off.** Waiting when it's time to act is the too-much side. Wait Well always asks "Is it time now?"
- **Being quiet and obedient.** A child who sits silently and never asks isn't waiting *well*. Asking once, kindly, and then waiting is the mean.
- **Keep Going, Pause Button** and **Better Together:** see "Which strength is it?" above.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 4, Parts 4–6: waiting out the snow, and waiting for the thaw that the snowdrop finale answers.
- **Season 1 seeds for "remember when"** (good choices and recoveries only, rule 14): Chapter 27, holding on in the old alder until the water sinks (waiting well). Chapter 34, after the knots, Hugh holds Clara while she cries for Grandma and doesn't hurry her.
- **A face for the put-it-off side, not a "remember when":** Reeve Osric's "We'll see to it tomorrow," which he says sorry for in Chapter 59. It's a grown-up's moment, so Ember never hands it to a child.

### 7. One Thing

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | One Thing |
| **Grown-up name** | Focus and attention |
| **Kid line** | I put the rest down and do this |
| **Family · season** | Steady (Temperance) · Season 4, Parts 7–9 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **One Thing** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Scattered:** hopping from thing to thing, and leaving everything half done. | Putting the rest down and doing this one thing, then looking up when it's time or when someone needs you. | **Stuck on it:** so stuck on your thing that you don't hear anyone, can't stop when it's time, or don't notice someone who needs help. |
| **Grown-up words** | Distractibility | Focused attention that can also shift when it should. | Fixation (rigid over-focus) |

**Tag:** `One Thing · between scattered and stuck on it`
**Sides:** too_little = scattered · too_much = stuck on it
*The pair is ours.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "One thing now."
- **Gesture:** Brush both hands out to the sides, as if setting everything else down. Then hold both hands flat beside your eyes, like a cart-horse's blinkers, and look straight ahead.
- **Little gesture:** blinkers: both hands flat beside the eyes.

**Tuesday pair** (example): a beacon-walker who starts to stack the wood, then feeds the goats, then starts a snowman, and leaves all three half done (scattered) / a boy so busy whittling a spoon that he doesn't hear his little sister calling that the fire has burned low (stuck on it). Both are helped back to the one thing that matters now.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. Before you start something new, put the last thing away. (Part 7)
2. Put the other things out of sight (the toy, the screen, the noise) and do just this one thing until it's done. (Part 8)
3. When someone speaks to you, put your thing down, turn and look at them. Then go back to it. (Part 9: this one guards against the stuck-on-it side.)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can finish one small thing before I start the next. | She puts the puzzle away before getting out the play-dough. She sits for a whole short picture book. She builds one tower right to the top before running off. | If it's time for something new, then I put the last thing away. |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can put the other things down and do one job until it's done, for about ten minutes. | He tidies all the blocks without stopping to play with them. He finishes one whole drawing, colored in. He listens to a whole chapter without getting up. He says "I'm busy with this, then I'll come." | If something else calls me, then I say "One thing now" and finish my job first. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can do one thing for twenty minutes with distractions put away, and bring myself back when my mind wanders. | They do their homework with the tablet in another room. They read for twenty minutes. They notice they've drifted and say "Back to it" without being told. They practice one piece, not three at once. | If my mind wanders off, then I notice, say "Back to it," and start where I stopped. |
| **Stretch** | I can choose the one thing that matters most right now, even if it means putting down something fun. | She stops her game straight away when a sibling is hurt. She picks which of two jobs to do first and says why. She puts her book down and looks at you when you speak. | If someone needs me, then I put my thing down, look at them, and come back to it later. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* They put all the blocks back in the box before asking for the crayons. They sit through two short picture books in a row.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* He colors in one whole picture with the tablet put away, about ten minutes. He notices he's wandered off from tidying and comes back to it without being told.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** something else calls me · my mind wanders off · someone needs me
- **Then I…** say "One thing now" and finish first · say "Back to it" · put it down and look at them

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables:** Aesop, *The Milkmaid and Her Pail*: she's so busy daydreaming about what the milk will buy that she tosses her head and spills it. Aesop, *The Cat and the Fox*: the fox boasts of a hundred tricks, the cat knows one (climb a tree). When the hounds come, the cat is up the tree at once while the fox is still deciding. (Retell it cozily: the hounds only bark, and the fox gets a muddy run. Ember will have opinions.)
- **Stoic** (paraphrase): concentrate every minute on doing what's in front of you, well and willingly, and set other things aside (Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 2.5). Also: "Everywhere means nowhere." (Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 2, Richard Gummere's translation)
- **Arthurian:** the quest for the Grail. Many knights set out; most turned aside to other adventures or went home. Galahad, Percival and Bors kept to the one quest and saw it through (Malory, *Le Morte d'Arthur*, Books XIII–XVII). Tell it as a quest for a cup of light, with no fighting.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* Mary and Martha (Luke 10:38–42). Martha is busy with many things; Mary sits and listens. "Martha, Martha, thou art careful and troubled about many things: But one thing is needful." (Luke 10:41–42, KJV) Martha is loved, not scolded: she is invited to put the rest down.
  - *Weekly verse:* "This one thing I do." (Philippians 3:13, KJV)

**Books that pair well**
- *The Listening Walk* by Paul Showers, illustrated by Aliki. A girl and her dad go for a walk and do nothing but listen. Little and Middle.
- *Owl Moon* by Jane Yolen, illustrated by John Schoenherr. Owling on a winter night takes quiet, full attention. Middle and Big. It suits the Season 4 winter.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Stuck on it vs stubborn.** Keep Going's stubborn is using the same *method* again and again. One Thing's stuck-on-it is not being able to *look up*. A child who can't hear his sister crying because of his game is stuck on it.
- **Curious** (Season 3). Hopping from wonder to wonder is good at play time. One Thing is for when there's a job to finish. Never frame curiosity as "scattered."
- **Screens.** A child glued to a screen is not showing One Thing. Choosing to put the screen away is.
- **Keep Going:** see "Which strength is it?" above.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 4, Parts 7–9: long dark days, one job at a time, and the beacon-walker's one job (keep the fire fed on your night) that can't be dropped for something more fun.
- **Season 1 seeds:** Chapter 20, "First the job, then the fun." Chapter 26, Alfie counting and chalking every one of a hundred stones. Chapter 42, Hugh reading the old letters sound by sound.

### 8. Enough

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Enough |
| **Grown-up name** | Contentment and moderation |
| **Kid line** | Happy with what I have |
| **Family · season** | Steady (Temperance) · Season 4, Parts 10–12 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Enough** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Going without:** saying "I don't want any" when you do, not taking your fair share of turns or toys, not letting yourself enjoy good things. | Glad of what you have, stopping at enough, and sharing the rest. | **Greedy:** "More! More!" Grabbing, keeping it all, never happy with what you've got. |
| **Grown-up words** | Self-denial: refusing your fair share, or refusing it for show | Contentment and moderation: glad of enough, and generous with the rest. | Greed, envy and hoarding |

**Tag:** `Enough · between going without and greedy`
**Sides:** too_little = going without · too_much = greedy
*The pair is ours. It borrows the shape of Aristotle's temperance, the mean between self-indulgence and "insensibility" (Nicomachean Ethics III.10–12). But he limits temperance to the bodily pleasures of touch and taste (III.10), and Enough deliberately leaves food and bodies out (see "The food rule" below). What's left (things, turns, screen time) isn't what his temperance covers, so never call this Aristotle's pair.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "Enough for me, and some to share." (Little: "I have enough!")
- **Gesture:** Hold your hands a little way apart, palms facing, as if showing "this much": enough for me. Then move one hand out toward someone: some to share.
- **Little gesture:** hands a little way apart: "this much".

**Tuesday pair** (example): a man who fills his barn with far more firewood than his family could burn all winter, while his neighbors' woodpiles run out (greedy) / an old woman who won't let the children stack wood by her door ("I don't need anything"), though her own pile is gone and her fire is low (going without). He shares his spare wood; she lets herself be given to, and says thank you. *No Tuesday pair is about food (see "The food rule").*

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. When you've had your turn, say "That's enough for me, thank you." (Part 10)
2. When someone else gets something nice, say "Good for you!" instead of "What about me?" (Part 11)
3. When you have more than you need, give some away: a toy, a turn, a sticker. (Part 12)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can say "That's enough for me" and "Thank you." | He takes one sticker when you pass the sheet round. He stops asking for "more" after you say it's the last turn on the swing, even with a small grumble. He gives one of his toy cars to his sister. | If you say "Last turn!", then I say "I have enough!" |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can be happy with what I got, even when someone else got something different, and share some. | They don't compare party bags out loud. They say "I have enough" when choosing. They don't ask for something in every shop. They share their crayons without being asked. | If someone gets something I don't, then I say "Good for you!" and think of one thing I have. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can stop at enough by myself (screen time, toys, turns) and notice what I already have. | She turns off the screen when the agreed time is up, without a fight. She chooses not to ask for something at the shop. She names three things she's glad she has. She picks out a toy she's outgrown to give away. | If I want to keep going past the time we agreed, then I say "That's enough for now" and stop. |
| **Stretch** | I can give some of what I have to someone who has less, and be glad, not jealous, when a friend gets something great. | He chooses toys or books to give away himself. He says "Cool!" and means it when a friend shows off a new bike. He saves part of his pocket money to give. | If I have more than I need, then I choose some to share. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* She says "Good for you!" when her brother opens a present, with a little help. She gives a friend one of her stickers.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* They stop the cartoon when the timer rings, with one reminder. They choose one old toy to give to a younger cousin.

**The food rule.** Enough is about wanting more *things*, *turns* and *screen time*. It is never about food: not how much a child eats at a meal, not sweets or treats, not tasting. It is never about bodies or weight. The child decides how much they're hungry for (the feeding approach known as Ellyn Satter's "division of responsibility"). No rung, mission or story line praises a child for eating less, and the gesture never touches the tummy. In Season 4, what is shared is always extra (see the Steady family intro).

**If-then cards**
- **When…** it's the last turn · someone gets something I don't · I have lots
- **Then I…** say "I have enough!" · say "Good for you!" · choose some to share

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and fairy tales:** Aesop, *The Dog and His Reflection*: a dog with a bone sees "another dog" with a bigger bone in the water, snaps at it, and drops his own. Aesop, *The Boy and the Filberts*: his fist full of nuts is stuck in the jar; take half, and his hand comes out. (Retell it with marbles or buttons in the jar, so it isn't about food.) Grimm, *The Fisherman and His Wife*: a magic fish grants wish after wish, and the wife is never content, until they're back in their hovel. *The Ant and the Grasshopper* (canon's Season 4 echo), retold with mercy: the ants have enough, and they share with the grasshopper.
- **Stoic** (canon: "Seneca on having enough"): "It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor." (Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 2, Richard Gummere's translation)
- **Homer:** Eumaeus the swineherd has very little, yet he gives a ragged stranger the best of what he has, and says that a small gift can still be a dear one (*Odyssey* 14; paraphrase). The same good old man is the children's echo in Season 1, Part 10.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story* (canon: "Joseph and the lean years"): Joseph tells Pharaoh to store grain in the seven good years, so there is enough for the seven lean years, and even his hungry brothers are fed (Genesis 41–42). *Also:* the manna in the wilderness, where "he that gathered much had nothing over, and he that gathered little had no lack" (Exodus 16:18, KJV).
  - *Weekly verse:* "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content." (Philippians 4:11, ESV)

**Books that pair well**
- *Pete the Cat and His Four Groovy Buttons* by Eric Litwin, illustrated by James Dean. His buttons pop off one by one, and he's still glad of what he has. Little and Middle. (It replaces Mo Willems's *Should I Share My Ice Cream?*, because Enough stays away from food.)
- *Those Shoes* by Maribeth Boelts. Jeremy wants the shoes everyone has, gets a pair that doesn't fit, and gives them to a friend. Middle and Big.
- *The Quiltmaker's Gift* by Jeff Brumbeau, illustrated by Gail de Marcken. A king who has everything learns to give it away. Big.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Going without.** "I don't want any" when you do is the too-little side. Taking your fair share and enjoying it is Enough.
- **"It's fine."** Enough is not pretending things are fine when they aren't. Alfie's "It's fine, it's enough!" at the empty fire-bowl (Chapter 52) is a Truth-Teller moment, because it wasn't enough.
- **Thankful** and **Fair Play** (Season 2). Thankful looks at who *gave* it. Fair Play shares turns and a say. Enough is about *wanting*: stopping at enough and sharing what's left over.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 4, Parts 10–12, the season's end. The stores must last until spring, and the children see when they have enough and share the rest with snowed-in neighbors (canon section 6). The thaw and the snowdrop close it.
- **Season 1 seeds for "remember when"** (good choices only, rule 14): Chapter 18, Clara saved her slice of honey-bread "for Grandma", and it helped her remember home. Tell it as a gift kept for someone she loves, never as eating less.
- **The greedy side, played for a laugh:** Ember's greed for sausages and cheese rinds, a running joke that carries the greedy side without shaming a child. It's the only greedy-side seed. Never use a child's eating from Season 1 (such as Alfie's three slices of honey-bread in Chapter 16) as the greedy side.

## The Kind & Fair family (Justice) · Season 2 · "The Broken Bridge"

The canon's summary (section 6): spring comes to the Westering. The spring floods have drowned both old fords, and the old stone bridge over the Swale at Twyford has fallen. The children listen to both sides, share the work fairly, find the mason's mark on each old stone and match the marks to families on both banks, and get two stubborn villages working as one. Cuthbert forges the irons, Wistan carries the stones, and Edith's geese carry messages. The canon's echoes: Aesop's *Two Goats* and *The Four Oxen and the Lion*, the Round Table where no seat is higher, and Solomon's judgment (faith toggle). **Finale:** both villages build out from their own banks and meet in the middle, where the children set the keystone and hang a lantern lit from the Wardlow beacon.

|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Cold:** not noticing, or noticing and walking past. "Not my problem." | Notice how they feel, ask, then help the way they want. | **Pushy:** helping without asking, taking over, hugging someone who doesn't want a hug, doing it *for* them. |
| **Grown-up words** | Callousness or indifference | Compassion: care that pays attention to what the other person needs. | Intrusive or smothering help that serves the helper; rescuing too soon. |

**Tag:** `Kind · between cold and pushy` (canon section 8, kept as is: see "The Kind pair" at the top)
**Sides:** too_little = cold · too_much = pushy
*The pair is ours, not Aristotle's. He does say pity is among the feelings that "may be felt both too much and too little" (Nicomachean Ethics II.6, Ross), which is the shape of cold and pushy. But he never names that virtue or its two vices. His nearest named mean is friendliness, between the flatterer and the quarrelsome, surly person (IV.6).*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "I see you. Can I help?" (Little: "Can I help?")
- **Gesture:** Pat your own arm gently twice (I see you), then hold out one open hand, palm up (Can I help?).
- **Little gesture:** one open palm, held out.
- The "Can I help?" is what keeps it from being pushy: you ask first.

**Tuesday pair** (example): a villager who steps round a crying child to get to market (cold) / a well-meaning aunt who keeps scooping up a lamb that's struggling to stand, so it never learns to stand at all (pushy). The villager comes back; the aunt sets the lamb down and lets it try.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. When someone is sad or hurt, go close, ask "Are you okay?", and wait for the answer. (Part 1)
2. Ask "Can I help?" before you help, and then help *their* way. (Part 2)
3. When someone is left out, give them a place or a job: "Come sit with us," or "You carry the bell." (Part 3)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can notice when someone is sad and bring them something. | They pat a crying brother or sister. They bring a blanket or teddy to someone who's hurt. They tell you, "He's sad." They take the teddy back when someone says "no thanks." | If someone is crying, then I ask "Can I help?" |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can ask "Can I help?" and help the way they want, even with someone I don't know well. | She asks a new child at school to play. She asks her brother "Hug, or space?" and does what he says. She helps a friend pick up spilled crayons without taking over. She tells you when a classmate was sad and what she did. | If I see someone on their own, then I say "Do you want to play with us?" |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can be kind when it costs me something: give up my turn, stop my game, or stay with someone others leave out. | He leaves his game to sit with a child who fell. He gives the front seat to a sad sibling without being asked. He includes a child the group usually skips. He sees a grown-up with full arms and holds the door. | If someone gets left out, then I go and stand with them, even if my friends don't. |
| **Stretch** | I can be kind to someone I find hard, and kind when nobody sees or says thanks. | They speak kindly to a sibling who was mean to them earlier. They do a secret kindness (make a sibling's bed) and don't tell. They comfort a friend by listening instead of fixing. | If someone was unkind to me, then I still use kind words, and I tell a grown-up if it keeps happening. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* He asks "Can I help?" when a friend drops the crayons, and helps pick them up. He asks "Hug?" before hugging, and waits for the answer.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* She stops her game to help a younger child who fell at the park, and stays until a grown-up comes. She lets a sad friend have the first turn on the swing.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** someone is crying · someone is on their own · I want to help
- **Then I…** say "Are you okay?" and wait · say "Come sit with us" · ask "Can I help?" first

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables:** Aesop, *The Lion and the Mouse* (the smallest helps the biggest; the retold shelf already tags it Kind). Aesop, *The Dove and the Ant* (a leaf dropped to a drowning ant; kindness comes back). Aesop, *The North Wind and the Sun* (gentle warmth does what force can't: the pushy side).
- **Stoic:** Seneca, *On the Happy Life* 24: *"Ubicumque homo est, ibi beneficii locus est."* In plain English: "Wherever there is a human being, there is room for a kindness." (The Latin is exact; the English is ours.)
- **Homer:** *Iliad* 6. Hector's baby son is frightened by the horsehair crest on his father's helmet and cries. Hector laughs, takes the helmet off, lays it on the ground, and holds him. A big person notices how a small one feels and changes what *he* is doing. Retell only the helmet; leave out the war around it.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30–37). At bedtime: a traveler was hurt and left by the road. Nobody is shown hurting him, and the robbers are never on the page. Two people pass by (cold). The Samaritan stops, cares for the man, brings him to an inn and pays for his care. Jesus ends: "You go, and do likewise" (Luke 10:37, ESV). The Homeschool screen already names it for Kind & Fair season (canon section 8).
  - *Weekly verse:* "Be kind to one another, tenderhearted" (Ephesians 4:32, ESV).

**Books that pair well**
- *The Rabbit Listened* by Cori Doerrfeld. Every animal pushes a fix on Taylor, and the rabbit just listens. The pushy side, shown gently. Little and Middle.
- *How to Heal a Broken Wing* by Bob Graham. The only person on a busy street who notices a hurt pigeon. Little and Middle.
- *The Invisible Boy* by Trudy Ludwig, illustrated by Patrice Barton. One small act of including someone. Middle and Big.

**What it gets confused with**
- **"Nice"** (saying yes to everything). That's the pushy side turned on yourself. Kind can say "no" kindly.
- **Truth-Teller.** A kind child still tells the truth. Kind decides *how* to say it (canon Part 11: "Say the hard true thing kindly").
- **Fair Play:** see "Which strength is it?" above. Giving your turn away is kind, and nobody has to do it.

**Where it sits**

Chapter 28, Clara holds out her hand to the little dragon, *and waits*. Chapter 44, Clara wraps soggy Ember in the fleece herself. Clara's pocket question is "Who needs help?"
- **Comes back:** Season 4, where the children share with snowed-in neighbors.

### 10. Fair Play

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Fair Play |
| **Grown-up name** | Fairness |
| **Kid line** | Everyone gets a turn and a say |
| **Family · season** | Kind & Fair (Justice) · Season 2, Parts 4–6 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Fair Play** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Hogging:** taking more than your share, cutting in, changing the rules so you win, never letting anyone else choose. | Everyone gets a turn and a say, including you, including the littlest. | **Scorekeeping:** counting every crumb, "That's not FAIR!" over tiny things, telling on others to win, no give at all. |
| **Grown-up words** | Injustice and grasping: taking too much. | Justice: giving each their due. | Rigid legalism: insisting on exact equality with no mercy or common sense. |

**Tag:** `Fair Play · between hogging and scorekeeping`
**Sides:** too_little = hogging · too_much = scorekeeping
*The pair is ours. It borrows Aristotle's ideas, not his pair. He calls justice a mean, but "not in the same way as the other virtues" (Nicomachean Ethics V.5, W. D. Ross's translation). Just action lies between "acting unjustly and being unjustly treated; for the one is to have too much and the other to have too little" (V.5, Ross). Being unjustly treated is something you suffer, not a flaw in your character, so only the hogging side is a failing in his account. For the scorekeeping side we borrow his equity (V.10): the fair person "is no stickler for his rights in a bad sense but tends to take less than his share though he has the law on his side" (Ross). "Scorekeeping" is our kid word for being a stickler for your rights.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "Fair and square!"
- **Gesture:** Hold both hands out flat, palms up, like the two pans of a scale. Wobble them, then make them level.
- **Little gesture:** both hands out flat and level.

**Tuesday pair** (example): an Upton carter who takes the whole ford path and makes everyone else wait (hogging) / a Netherton man who won't lift a stone until someone proves exactly whose carts cracked the bridge (scorekeeping). The carter learns to take turns; the Netherton man lifts a stone first and argues later.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. Take turns out loud: "You go first, then me." Use a timer if it's hard. (Part 4)
2. When you share one thing, one person cuts and the other chooses. (Part 5)
3. Before deciding, ask the quietest person, "What do you think?" (Part 6)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can take turns, with a grown-up's help. | She waits for her turn on the slide. She hands over the toy when the timer rings. She says "your turn" in a simple game. | If someone else has the toy, then I ask "Can I have a turn?" |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can play by the rules when I'm losing, and share so everyone gets some. | He plays a whole board game fairly while he's behind and says "Good game." He splits a snack by cut-and-choose. He lets his little sister choose the game sometimes. He gives a turn to a child who hasn't had one. | If I'm losing, then I keep playing fair and say "Good game" at the end. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can make it fair for everyone, not just me: hear both sides and find a fair way. | They sort out a squabble by asking each child what happened. They own up when they broke a rule themselves. They make a rule that lets the youngest play (a head start). They notice when the same friend always picks last. | If two people want the same thing, then I ask both what they want and offer a fair way: take turns, cut-and-choose, or a timer. |
| **Stretch** | I can tell when "the same" isn't fair, speak up for someone getting less, and let a tiny unfairness to me go. | She gives the little one an easier rule without being asked. She speaks up kindly when a friend is left out. She shrugs off one extra go for her brother: "It's fine." | If it's unfair to someone else, then I say so kindly. If it's a tiny unfair to me, then I let it go. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* He plays a whole round of a simple matching game and says "Good game" with a little help. He hands the scooter over when the timer rings, without being asked twice.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* They share out the blocks so each child gets the same pile. When two friends want the same swing, they suggest "You first, then her, and I'll count."

**If-then cards**
- **When…** we both want it · I'm losing · someone hasn't had a say
- **Then I…** say "You go first, then me" · keep playing fair and say "Good game" · ask "What do you think?"

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and folk tales:** Aesop, *The Lion's Share* (the lion takes every portion: hogging). *The Two Greedy Bears*, a Hungarian folk tale: two cubs so busy making their halves of a cheese exactly equal that a fox nibbles away the whole thing (scorekeeping). Aesop, *The Two Goats* (canon, Season 2): neither will let the other cross the narrow way first, and both fall in the stream.
- **Aristotle:** just action lies between "acting unjustly and being unjustly treated; for the one is to have too much and the other to have too little" (*Nicomachean Ethics* V.5, Ross).
- **Arthurian** (canon, Season 2): the Round Table. In Wace's *Roman de Brut* (1155), Arthur has a round table made so that none of his knights can boast of sitting higher than another. No head of the table: everyone gets a say. *Homer, for grown-ups:* at the chariot race in *Iliad* 23, young Antilochus cuts Menelaus off, then owns it and offers back the prize, and Menelaus gives it to him anyway.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* Abram and Lot (Genesis 13:8–9). Their herders quarrel over grazing, and Abram, the elder, lets Lot choose first: "Let there be no strife, I pray thee, between me and thee… for we be brethren" (Genesis 13:8, KJV).
  - *Solomon's judgment* (canon, Season 2; 1 Kings 3:16–28) is about a wise judge hearing both sides. At bedtime the king only asks his testing question. No sword is ever drawn on the page. See flag 9.
  - *Weekly verse:* "So whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them" (Matthew 7:12, ESV).

**Books that pair well**
- *The Doorbell Rang* by Pat Hutchins. Twelve cookies, and more friends at the door each time. Little and Middle.
- *It's Mine!* by Leo Lionni. Three frogs quarrel over their island until a storm teaches them to share it. Little and Middle.
- *Two Greedy Bears* by Mirra Ginsburg, illustrated by Jose Aruego and Ariane Dewey. The scorekeeping side, and it's funny. Little and Middle.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Kind.** Fair is the floor, and kind can go above it.
- **Truth-Teller.** Telling on someone to *win* is scorekeeping (and blurting). Telling a grown-up because someone is being hurt is neither.
- **Better Together:** see "Which strength is it?" above. Sharing out the *work* fairly is where they meet.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 2, Parts 4–6. Upton blames Netherton, Netherton blames Upton, and the children listen to both sides and share the work fairly (canon section 6).
- **Season 1 seeds:** Chapter 59, the children name everyone who helped. Chapter 60, the village agrees to take turns walking wood to Wardlow every year.
- **Comes back:** Season 4's beacon turns, which keep the Chapter 60 promise.

### 11. Thankful

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Thankful |
| **Grown-up name** | Gratitude |
| **Kid line** | I see the good things people do for me |
| **Family · season** | Kind & Fair (Justice) · Season 2, Parts 7–9 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Thankful** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Grabby:** taking it and running, without seeing who made it, fixed it or gave it. "Is that all?" | See the good thing *and* the person who did it, and say so truly. | **Gushy:** thank-yous bigger than true, to please someone or to get more. ("You're the BEST grandma in the WORLD. Can I have another?") |
| **Grown-up words** | Ingratitude and entitlement | Gratitude: noticing a gift and honoring the giver. | Flattery: thanks used as a tool. Also the anxious debtor who can't rest until he's paid it back. |

**Tag:** `Thankful · between grabby and gushy`
**Sides:** too_little = grabby · too_much = gushy
*The pair is ours. The gushy side is close to Aristotle's flatterer (Nicomachean Ethics IV.6). Seneca's On Benefits covers both sides; in Book 4 he says that someone in a desperate hurry to pay back a favor is thinking like a debtor, not a grateful friend (paraphrase). Gushy is also the Truth-Teller mean again: not bigger, not smaller.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "Thank you for…!" Always name the thing: "Thank you for my milk!"
- **Gesture:** Touch the fingertips of one flat hand to your chin, then move the hand out toward the person you're thanking. It's the sign for "thank you" in American Sign Language, so many toddlers already know it.
- **Little gesture:** the same. It's one move.
- *Why not a flame:* a child's gesture never holds, cups or lifts a flame, because the bible's fire-safety check fails any child holding fire, and children copy gestures. Brave already lifts an invisible lantern, so Thankful doesn't lift anything. The Season 1 world rule ("A thank-you makes a flame stand a little taller", canon section 1) stays in the story: the narrator can show a flame standing taller when someone says thank you, with nobody touching it.

**Tuesday pair** (example): a boy who grabs his bread from the ferry-woman's basket and runs off without a look (grabby) / a man who showers the mason with thank-yous so loud and long that he's plainly after a free stone for his own wall (gushy). The boy runs back; the man says one true thank-you and carries a stone himself.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. Say thank you *with the "for"*: "Thank you for fixing my bike." (Part 7)
2. At bedtime, name one good thing someone did for you today. (Part 8)
3. Thank someone for a job you usually don't see: the cook, the bus driver, whoever washed your clothes. (Part 9)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can say "Thank you for…" and name the thing. | "Thank you for my milk." He hugs Grandma and says "Thank you for the book." At bedtime he names one good thing. | If someone gives me something, then I say "Thank you for [the thing]!" |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can notice good things people do for me, without being told to say thanks. | They thank their teacher or the bus driver on their own. They thank kindly and truly for a present they didn't love: "Thank you for thinking of me." They name two good things at bedtime. They draw a thank-you picture on their own. | If someone does something for me, then I say "Thank you for…" before I run off. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can thank people for the jobs I don't usually see, and show thanks by doing something back. | She writes or draws a thank-you note herself. She thanks whoever cooked dinner. She notices the packed lunch or the clean kit and says so. She helps with a job as her thank-you. | If I notice a job someone did for me, then I thank them and do one thing to help back. |
| **Stretch** | I can be thankful for those who came before me, and thankful on a hard day. | He asks a grandparent for a story from long ago and thanks them. On a disappointing day he still finds three good things. He thanks a sibling. He thanks without wanting anything back. | If the day went wrong, then I still find three good things before I sleep. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* She says "Thank you for the ride!" to the bus driver without a reminder. She names one good thing at bedtime all by herself.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* He draws a thank-you picture for whoever washed his clothes. He carries his plate to the sink as a thank-you for dinner.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** someone gives me something · someone does a job for me · it's bedtime
- **Then I…** say "Thank you for…" and name it · thank them before I run off · name one good thing someone did today

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and fairy tales:** Grimm, *The Elves and the Shoemaker*: the shoemaker's wife says they should show the little men they're grateful, and the family sews them tiny clothes. Thanks shown by doing. *Androcles and the Lion*, retold: Androcles pulls a thorn from a lion's paw, and when they meet again long after, the lion remembers and is gentle and friendly to him. Tell only the thorn and the kind meeting again. Never the arena, and never a man thrown to a lion. Grimm, *The Old Man and His Grandson*: a child's wooden bowl reminds his parents to honor the grandfather. Gratitude to those who came before.
- **Stoic:** "From my grandfather Verus I learned good morals and the government of my temper." (Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 1.1, George Long's translation) The whole of Book 1 thanks, by name, everyone who taught him. The canon uses Book 1 in Season 1, Part 9, so this echoes it.
- **Homer:** *Odyssey* 8. As Odysseus leaves, Nausicaa, who found and helped him on the shore, asks him to remember her. He answers that he'll honor her all his days, because she saved him (paraphrase).
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* the ten lepers (Luke 17:11–19). Ten are healed, and only one comes back to say thank you. "Were there not ten cleansed? but where are the nine?" (Luke 17:17, KJV) At bedtime, call it "a sickness that made people keep away."
  - *Weekly verse:* "Give thanks in all circumstances" (1 Thessalonians 5:18, ESV).

**Books that pair well**
- *Bear Says Thanks* by Karma Wilson, illustrated by Jane Chapman. Bear has nothing to share, and learns he can give thanks. Little.
- *Thank You, Omu!* by Oge Mora. Omu shares her stew with the whole street, and at night the street comes back to thank her. Little and Middle.
- *Last Stop on Market Street* by Matt de la Peña, illustrated by Christian Robinson. A grandmother teaches CJ to see the good around him. Middle and Big.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Politeness.** "Say thank you" on cue is manners. Thankful is *noticing*. The test: can the child say what the thanks is *for*?
- **Kind.** Being kind back is one way to show thanks, but thanks comes first: see it, and say it.
- **Enough:** see "Which strength is it?" above.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 2, Parts 7–9. The children find the mason's mark on each old bridge stone and trace the marks to families still living on both banks (canon section 6): thanks to those who built it first.
- **Season 1 seeds:** Chapter 4, they thank the flame and it stands a little taller. Chapter 18, the slice of honey-bread saved "for Grandma." Chapter 41, the Keepers' Stones. Chapter 60, the names on the lantern, and Hild's worn name cut fresh.

### 12. Better Together

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Better Together |
| **Grown-up name** | Teamwork |
| **Kid line** | We can do more side by side |
| **Family · season** | Kind & Fair (Justice) · Season 2, Parts 10–12 (the finale) |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Better Together** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Going it alone:** "I'll do it ALL myself, and nobody else touches it!" Won't let anyone help or join in, bosses the job, "my way or nothing." | Everyone does a part, including me, and we help each other. | **Leaving it to others:** "Someone else will do it." Tagging along, doing nothing, wandering off in the middle. |
| **Grown-up words** | Isolation or domineering: not letting others share the work or the say. | Cooperation: shared work toward a shared goal, each doing a real part. | Passivity or free-riding: counting on the group to carry you. |

**Tag:** `Better Together · between going it alone and leaving it to others`
**Sides:** too_little = going it alone · too_much = leaving it to others
*Never use the youngest's own catchphrase as the going-it-alone line. Clara's "Me do it!" is a sunny strength in the canon (rule 6).*
*The pair is ours. The dial is how much you lean on the team: too little, and you go it alone; too much, and you leave it all to them. Both failings are already in the canon. "It's a long way. Someone else will go" is why the beacon went out (leaving it to others). "A beacon kept by one person goes out" is what Hild learned (going it alone). Grandma says the mean in one line: "Nobody's too small to carry a light. But nobody carries it alone."*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "Side by side!"
- **Gesture:** Lace your fingers together and squeeze. One finger bends; ten laced together don't. It's the Bundle of Sticks, in two hands.
- **Little gesture:** clasp your hands together.

**Tuesday pair** (example): a young mason who insists on setting every stone himself and won't let anyone near the mortar (going it alone) / a row of villagers sitting on the bank, watching and calling out advice (leaving it to others). The mason hands someone a trowel; the watchers roll up their sleeves.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. Say "Let's do it together," and give everyone a part: "You hold, I'll carry." (Part 10)
2. When you're stuck, ask: "Can you hold this end?" (Part 11)
3. Do your own part all the way, even when nobody is watching. One stick each. (Part 12)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can do my part in a family job. | They carry the napkins while others set the table. They put their own toys in the box during the family tidy. They hold one end of a sheet while you fold it. | If everyone's tidying, then I put my toys away too. |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can let others help me, help them, and stay with the job until it's done. | She builds a tower with her brother, taking turns with the blocks. She lets a friend add to her drawing. She asks "Can you hold this?" instead of giving up. She stays through the whole family tidy-up without wandering off. | If I'm stuck, then I ask "Can you help me?" |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can help a team work: give everyone a real part, the littlest too, and finish mine. | He organizes his siblings to tidy a room, with a fair part each. He gives the youngest a real job, not a pretend one. He finishes his part when others stop. He tells you what someone else did well. | If a job is big, then I say "Let's do it together" and give everyone a part. |
| **Stretch** | I can help a stuck or squabbling team get going again, and give the credit away. | They settle a fight over who does what by suggesting turns. They teach a younger child how to do their part. They say "My sister found the last piece." They take the job nobody wants. | If my team is arguing, then I say "What are we trying to do?" and help each of us pick a part. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* He holds the dustpan while you sweep, all the way to the end. He asks "Help me?" when the lid is stuck, instead of throwing it.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* She gives her little brother the job of handing her the blocks, and builds the tower with him. She finishes putting her toys away when the others have stopped.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** the job is big · I'm stuck · others are working
- **Then I…** say "Let's do it together" · ask "Can you hold this end?" · ask "What can I do?"

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and folk tales:** *The Enormous Turnip*, a Russian folk tale: it takes everyone, down to the mouse. The retold shelf already tags it Better Together (canon section 8). Aesop, *The Four Oxen and the Lion* (canon, Season 2): safe while they stand together, not once they quarrel; the lion gives up and goes home. Grimm, *The Bremen Town Musicians*: four old animals no one wants find a home by working together (the robbers are only scared off).
- **Stoic:** "For we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth." (Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 2.1, George Long's translation)
- **Homer:** *Iliad* 10, around line 224. Diomedes asks for a companion: when two go together, one sees what's needed before the other, and one mind alone is slower (paraphrase). Aristotle quotes the same line about friendship at the start of *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII. Use only the saying, not the night raid it comes from.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* Nehemiah rebuilds the wall of Jerusalem, and each family repairs the piece nearest its own house (Nehemiah 3): "So built we the wall… for the people had a mind to work" (Nehemiah 4:6, KJV). Use chapter 3 and 4:6 only; the rest of chapter 4 has threats and guards.
  - *Weekly verse:* "A threefold cord is not quickly broken" (Ecclesiastes 4:12, KJV). Three children, one cord. Alternative: "Two are better than one" (Ecclesiastes 4:9, KJV).

**Books that pair well**
- *The Gigantic Turnip* by Aleksei Tolstoy, illustrated by Niamh Sharkey. Little.
- *Swimmy* by Leo Lionni. Little fish swim together as one big fish. Little and Middle.
- *Roxaboxen* by Alice McLerran, illustrated by Barbara Cooney. Children build a whole town together out of stones and boxes. Middle and Big.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Going along with the crowd.** A team can be wrong. Staying with your team isn't the same as doing whatever it says: that's Open Mind's believe-anything, and sometimes it's Brave's job to say no.
- **Keep Going.** Asking for help isn't giving up. On the Keep Going ladder, asking for help is already part of the mean.
- **Fair Play** and **Wait Well:** see "Which strength is it?" above.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 2, Parts 10–12, the finale. Cuthbert forges the irons, Wistan carries the stones and Edith's geese carry messages. Both villages build out from their own banks and meet in the middle, where the children set the keystone (canon section 6).
- **Season 1 seeds:** Chapter 25, Clara finds the last stone for Hugh's wall. Chapter 50, Ned helps call the oxen in. Chapters 54–55, everyone's one stick on Wardlow.

## The Wise family (Prudence) · Season 3 · "The Silent Library"

The canon's summary (section 6): Hild learned the keepers' lore as a girl at the old library at Beechholt, where the Westering keeps its know-how and its true tales. The library has gone silent. Nobody asks questions there any more, so the words are fading from the pages, and the old librarian has stopped talking because no one asks her anything. Its doors open only to good questions, set as old English riddles in daylight. The children bring the pages back by wondering, thinking things through, changing their minds when they learn something new, and at the end writing something new: the true tale of the last light. The canon's echoes: Socrates and his questions, the old English riddle-books, and Solomon asking for wisdom (faith toggle). **Finale:** the great hall murmuring again, children reading aloud to grown-ups by lamplight, and the children's own book, *The Last Light of Candlemere*, on the shelf beside the oldest ones.

**Proposed order,** following the list in `decisions.md`: Parts 1–3 Curious, Parts 4–6 Think It Through, Parts 7–9 Open Mind, Parts 10–12 Make Something New. The canon's summary follows the same order.

### 13. Curious

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Curious |
| **Grown-up name** | Love of learning |
| **Kid line** | I wonder, I ask, I find out |
| **Family · season** | Wise (Prudence) · Season 3, Parts 1–3 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Curious** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Not bothered:** "Whatever." "Boring." Never asks, never looks closely, never finds out. | Wonder, ask, and find out, about things that are yours to find out. | **Nosy:** poking into what isn't yours, opening what you were told not to, asking and asking when someone says "That's private." |
| **Grown-up words** | Incuriosity | Love of learning, which medieval writers called studiousness. | Prying, or restless curiosity that grabs at everything and respects nothing. |

**Tag:** `Curious · between not bothered and nosy`
**Sides:** too_little = not bothered · too_much = nosy
*The pair isn't Aristotle's. It follows Thomas Aquinas, who sets the virtue of studiousness against both negligence and curiosity in the bad sense (Summa Theologiae II-II, questions 166–167). The canon has already shown the mean: "That's not mine to tell" (Grandma and Ember) is a kind limit on someone else's curiosity.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "I wonder… let's find out!"
- **Gesture:** Curl one hand into a spyglass and peek through it.
- **Little gesture:** the same. A curled hand held to one eye is one move.

**Tuesday pair** (example): a library boy who yawns at every shelf and says it's all old stuff (not bothered) / a girl who opens the librarian's private letters "just to see" (nosy). The boy finds one page that makes him gasp; the girl folds the letters back and asks the librarian a question instead.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. Ask one "why?" or "how does it work?" today, and find the answer together: a book, a grown-up, or trying it. (Part 1)
2. Look closely: find three things about something ordinary, like a leaf, a snail or a spoon. (Part 2)
3. Ask before you open, touch or peek: "Is it okay if I look?" (Part 3)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can ask "What's that?" and look closely. | She crouches to watch ants. She asks what things are called. She asks "why?" and waits for the answer. She asks before she touches Grandma's things. | If we find something new, then I ask "What's that?" |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can ask a question, make a guess, and help find the answer. | He asks "How does it work?" and then looks inside, tries it, or asks the librarian himself. He says what he thinks before finding out ("I think the ice will melt first"). He asks for a book about something he wondered. He asks "Can I look?" first. | If I wonder about something, then I make a guess and we find out together. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can follow a question for days: guess, look it up, test it, and tell what I found. | They keep a wonder list. They read a nonfiction book they chose themselves. They run a small test (which cup of water freezes first?). They tell you three things they learned. They ask a good question of an expert: the vet, the librarian, a grandparent. | If I wonder about something, then I write it on my wonder list and pick one to find out this week. |
| **Stretch** | I can ask about things I thought I already knew, and about people whose lives are different from mine, respectfully. | She asks a grandparent what life was like when they were small. She asks a follow-up question and listens to the answer. She checks something she was "sure" of. When someone says "That's private," she stops asking. | If someone says "I'd rather not say," then I stop asking and wonder about something else. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* They guess "Will it float?" before dropping a toy in the bath, then watch. They ask "Can I look?" before opening a box.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* He wonders where the snail goes at night, and you look it up together over a few days. He tells you two things he found out about snails.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** I see something new · I wonder why · I want to open or peek
- **Then I…** ask "What's that?" · make a guess and find out · ask "Is it okay if I look?"

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and tales:** Aesop, *The Astronomer*: a man so busy staring at the stars that he falls into a well (wonder, but look where you're going). *Goldilocks and the Three Bears*: in someone else's house, trying everything (the nosy side). Kipling, *The Elephant's Child* in *Just So Stories*: a young elephant full of questions, whose curiosity in the end gives him his trunk.
- **Aristotle:** "All men by nature desire to know." (*Metaphysics* I.1, W. D. Ross's translation)
- **Homer:** *Odyssey* 12. Odysseus wants to hear the Sirens, so he has his crew tie him to the mast and stop their own ears with wax. He finds out *and* keeps everyone safe. The canon used the Sirens in Season 1, Part 5, with ears stopped; here the grown-up notices the mast.
- **Arthurian:** Percival visits a sad old host and never asks "What ails you?", so nothing is mended. Later he learns to ask, and the host is glad again. Keep it to the sadness and the question: no wound, no Grail mystery.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* the boy Jesus in the temple (Luke 2:41–52). His parents find him "sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking them questions" (Luke 2:46, ESV). At bedtime, start where his parents find him; don't linger on the three days lost.
  - *Weekly verse:* "It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter." (Proverbs 25:2, KJV)
- *Canon tie-in:* the library's doors open only to good questions, set as riddles. The real riddles of the Old English *Exeter Book* are the grown-up echo, and Socrates and his questions are the Season 3 echo the canon names.

**Books that pair well**
- *Ada Twist, Scientist* by Andrea Beaty, illustrated by David Roberts. A girl who asks "why?" and then tests it. Little and Middle.
- *The Magic School Bus at the Waterworks* by Joanna Cole, illustrated by Bruce Degen. Questions followed all the way to the answer. Middle and Big.
- *The Boy Who Harnessed the Wind* (picture-book edition) by William Kamkwamba and Bryan Mealer, illustrated by Elizabeth Zunon. A boy reads library books and builds a windmill for his village. Big.

*Not* Curious George: the first book begins with George being captured from Africa, which is widely criticized.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Nosy.** The test: is it mine to know?
- **Distraction.** Flitting from thing to thing without ever finding out is not curiosity.
- **Open Mind** and **One Thing:** see "Which strength is it?" above.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 3, Parts 1–3. Nobody asks questions at Beechholt any more, so the words are fading, and the doors open only to good questions (canon section 6). Alfie, who asks "How does it work?", will be tempted to lead every week: share the lead (canon rule 9). Clara's curious act must be real, like noticing something small the boys missed.
- **Season 1 seeds:** Chapter 13, Alfie counts the flock and notices that Wistan pats only backs. Chapter 31, Alfie asks exactly how the wind-knots work. Chapter 46, he asks how the latch works.

### 14. Think It Through

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Think It Through |
| **Grown-up name** | Good judgment |
| **Kid line** | Stop, think, then choose the wise way (see flag 3) |
| **Family · season** | Wise (Prudence) · Season 3, Parts 4–6 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Think It Through** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Rushing:** doing the first thing that pops into your head, without thinking what happens next. | Think what happens next, pick the wise way, then do it. | **Dithering:** thinking round and round, "I don't know, I don't know," and never choosing, so the chance goes by. |
| **Grown-up words** | Haste: acting without deliberating. | Practical wisdom (prudence): deliberating well, then acting. | Indecision: deliberating that never ends in a choice. |

**Tag:** `Think It Through · between rushing and dithering`
**Sides:** too_little = rushing · too_much = dithering
*The pair is ours. The grown-up anchor is Aristotle on good deliberation: "men say that one should carry out quickly the conclusions of one's deliberation, but should deliberate slowly" (Nicomachean Ethics VI.9, Ross). Aquinas lists "precipitation" (rushing) among the failures of prudence (Summa Theologiae II-II, question 53).*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "What happens next?" (In full: "Think it through: what happens next?")
- **Gesture:** Tap your head twice, hold up two fingers (two ways), then fold one down (the one you choose).
- **Little gesture:** tap your head.

**Tuesday pair** (example): a boy who grabs the first book off the top shelf and brings the whole row down (rushing) / a girl who stands between two doors all afternoon, unable to pick, until the lamps go out (dithering). The children help him put the books back and plan how to reach the next one; they help her say two ways and pick.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. Before a big choice, ask "What happens next?" and say one thing that could happen. (Part 4)
2. Name two ways, then pick: "We could… or we could…" Hugh's pocket question fits here too: "What's the brave thing, and what's the silly thing?" (Part 5)
3. If you're stuck, count to ten and choose, or ask someone wise. (Part 6)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can tell you what might happen next when you ask, before I do it. | He pours slowly because "it will spill." He puts his coat on because it's cold out. He stops at something wobbly when you ask "What happens next?" and says "Fall down!" He picks between two cups without a meltdown. | If you ask "What happens next?", then I tell you my guess. |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can think of two ways and pick the one that works best, for everyone. | They say "We could… or we could…" They build on the floor, not on the wobbly table, and say why. They pick a game their little sister can play too. They ask a grown-up when they're truly not sure. | If I have to choose, then I say two ways and pick the one that works for everyone. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can plan before I start, think what happens next for me and for others, and decide in good time. | She plans a project in steps before she starts (what she needs, what comes first). She packs her own bag from a list. She thinks about how a joke will land before she tells it. She decides within a minute instead of dithering. | If it's a big choice, then I think of two ways and what happens next for each, and I pick in one minute. |
| **Stretch** | I can look back at how my choice went, choose better next time, and ask for advice. | After a plan goes wrong, he says what he'd change. He asks a grandparent for advice and weighs it. He turns down something fun now that will go badly later. He weighs a dare instead of just taking it. | If I'm not sure, then I ask someone wise, and then I decide. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* She says "Blocks or train?" and picks one. She carries the full cup with two hands and says why.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* They get out everything for a craft before they start (paper, glue, scissors). They choose which of two games to play in under a minute.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** I'm about to climb, pour or run · I have to choose · I can't decide
- **Then I…** ask "What happens next?" · say two ways and pick one · count to ten and choose, or ask someone wise

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and folk tales:** Aesop, *The Fox and the Goat*: the thirsty goat jumps into the well without thinking how he'll get out (rushing). Aesop, *The Two Frogs and the Well*: two frogs looking for water find a deep well; one wants to jump straight in, and the other asks how they'd get out if it dried up. *The Three Little Pigs*: the pig who plans and builds with brick (the wolf gives up and goes home).
- **Stoic:** "In every affair consider what precedes and follows, and then undertake it." (Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 29, Thomas Wentworth Higginson's translation) It's "What happens next?" in grown-up words.
- **Homer:** *Odyssey* 6, around lines 141–147. Shipwrecked and in need, Odysseus sees Nausicaa and weighs two ways: clasp her knees, or speak gently from a distance. He decides it's wiser to keep his distance and speak kindly, and it works (paraphrase). Two ways, one wise choice.
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* the wise and foolish builders (Matthew 7:24–27): "whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock" (Matthew 7:24, KJV).
  - *Weekly verse:* "Ponder the path of thy feet" (Proverbs 4:26, KJV).

**Books that pair well**
- *The Three Little Pigs* by James Marshall. The classic, and funny. Little and Middle.
- *Stuck* by Oliver Jeffers. Floyd throws bigger and bigger things into a tree to free his kite. It's rushing, and it's very funny. Little and Middle.
- *The Paper Bag Princess* by Robert Munsch, illustrated by Michael Martchenko. Elizabeth outthinks a dragon instead of fighting it. Middle and Big.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Pause Button** (Season 4). Pause Button stops a *feeling* before it takes over. Think It Through weighs *choices* and what happens next. A child may need Pause Button first, then Think It Through. See flag 3.
- **Brave.** Hugh's pocket question uses judgment in the service of courage. In Brave weeks it's tagged Brave; in Wise weeks the same question is tagged Think It Through.
- **Keep Going's stubborn.** Doing the same thing again and again is a Keep Going failing. Choosing badly in the first place is a Think It Through failing.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 3, Parts 4–6. The library's know-how (how to mend, grow, heal and build) is only useful if you think before you use it.
- **Season 1 seeds for "remember when"** (good choices and recoveries only, rule 14): Chapter 15, Hugh asks his pocket question and goes back to the gate the wise way. Chapter 34, after the knots, Hugh says "I untied them," Alfie asks "What else could we try?", and together they choose to turn west. *Never use Chapter 33 (the untying) as a callback: it's a mistake Hugh has already owned.*

### 15. Open Mind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Open Mind |
| **Grown-up name** | Humility (see flag 10) |
| **Kid line** | I might be wrong, and that's okay |
| **Family · season** | Wise (Prudence) · Season 3, Parts 7–9 |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Open Mind** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Know-it-all:** "I'm RIGHT." Won't listen, won't try another way, won't change even when shown. | Listen, check, and change your mind when you learn something true, and keep it when you don't. | **Believe-anything:** changing your mind for whoever is loudest or last, believing any big story without asking "How do you know?" |
| **Grown-up words** | Arrogance and closed-mindedness | Intellectual humility: open to learning, still anchored in what's true. | Credulity: no settled judgment of your own. |

**Tag:** `Open Mind · between know-it-all and believe-anything`
**Sides:** too_little = know-it-all · too_much = believe-anything
*The pair is ours. "Humility" here means being teachable, not thinking little of yourself. The canon's Season 3 summary names "changing their minds when they learn something new" as the Wise season's third step.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "I might be wrong. Tell me more!" (Little: "Tell me more!")
- **Gesture:** Press your palms together like a shut book, then open them wide like a book falling open.
- **Little gesture:** open your hands like a book.

**Tuesday pair** (example): an old scholar who won't hear that the map on the wall is upside down (know-it-all) / a gossip who believes and repeats every rumor, then believes the opposite by teatime (believe-anything). The scholar turns the map round and laughs; the gossip starts asking "How do you know?"

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. When someone disagrees, say "Tell me more," and listen to the end. (Part 7)
2. Say "I changed my mind" out loud when you learn something new, and be glad of it. (Part 8)
3. Before believing big news, ask "How do you know?" (Part 9)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can listen to someone else's idea and try it. | They let a brother or sister choose the game sometimes. They try someone else's way of building. They play a made-up game the other child's way. They say "Okay, your way" in play. | If someone tells me their idea, then I say "Tell me more!" |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can say "I might be wrong," try another way, and ask "How do you know?" | She tries a friend's way of building the ramp. She says "Oh! I didn't know that." She changes her guess when she sees what really happened. She asks "How do you know?" when a friend says something surprising. | If I'm sure I'm right, then I say "I might be wrong" and check. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can change my mind when I learn something new, say so out loud, and see things from someone else's side. | In an argument he says "Actually, you're right about that." He asks "What do you think she felt?" He checks before passing on a rumor. He fixes a schoolwork mistake when shown, without sulking. | If someone shows me I'm wrong, then I say "I changed my mind," and thank them. |
| **Stretch** | I can really listen to someone who disagrees and still stand by what's true, changing my mind for good reasons, not loud ones. | They let a sibling finish their side before answering. They say "I see why you think that, but I still think…" They won't go along with a group's wrong idea. They can say *why* they changed their mind. | If everyone else says something different, then I ask "How do we know?" before I change my mind. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* He says "Oh!" and changes his guess when the ball rolls the other way. He tries his sister's way of stacking the cups.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* She says "You're right, it is a moth, not a butterfly" when shown the picture. She asks "How do you know?" when a friend says the moon follows the car.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** someone disagrees · I'm sure I'm right · I hear big news
- **Then I…** say "Tell me more" and listen · say "I might be wrong" and check · ask "How do you know?"

**Classical echoes**
- **Fables and folk tales:** *Henny Penny* (also called *Chicken Licken*): one acorn falls, "The sky is falling!", and everyone believes it (believe-anything). Retell it with no fox dinner: they all go to tell the king, and the king shows them the acorn. *The Blind Men and the Elephant*, an old Indian parable: each man touches one part and is sure he knows the whole (know-it-all).
- **Stoic:** "If any man is able to convince me and show me that I do not think or act right, I will gladly change; for I seek the truth, by which no man was ever injured." (Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 6.21, George Long's translation) Grown-up extra: "it is impossible for a man to begin to learn that which he thinks that he knows" (Epictetus, *Discourses* 2.17.1, Long).
- **Arthurian:** Malory, *Le Morte d'Arthur*, Book VII, the tale of Sir Gareth. Lady Lynette scorns the young knight "Beaumains" all the way down the road as a mere kitchen boy. Deed by deed he proves her wrong, and in the end she owns she misjudged him (paraphrase).
- **Scripture** (faith toggle):
  - *Story:* Solomon asks for wisdom (1 Kings 3:5–12): "Give therefore thy servant an understanding heart to judge thy people, that I may discern between good and bad" (1 Kings 3:9, KJV). The Hebrew for "understanding heart" means, word for word, a *listening* heart. The Homeschool screen already names Solomon asking for wisdom for Wise season (canon section 8). Alternative: Naaman listens to his servants and changes his mind (2 Kings 5:9–14).
  - *Weekly verse:* "He that hearkeneth unto counsel is wise." (Proverbs 12:15, KJV, second half)

**Books that pair well**
- *Duck! Rabbit!* by Amy Krouse Rosenthal, illustrated by Tom Lichtenheld. One picture, two voices, both sure they're right. Little and Middle.
- *They All Saw a Cat* by Brendan Wenzel. One cat, seen a dozen ways. Little and Middle.
- *Seven Blind Mice* by Ed Young. The blind men and the elephant, told with mice. Only the last mouse, who explores the whole thing, gets it right. Middle and Big.

**What it gets confused with**
- **Truth-Teller.** Changing your mind isn't fibbing. "I was wrong" is Truth-Teller and Open Mind together (see the rule under "Which strength is it?").
- **Giving in to be liked.** Dropping what's true so people will like you is the believe-anything side, not Kind.
- **Curious:** see "Which strength is it?" above.

**Where it sits**
- **First taught:** Season 3, Parts 7–9. As the pages come back, some old things the children were sure of turn out to be different. Keep it gentle: the mistaken belief is always an honest one, and nobody is made a fool of.
- **Season 1 seeds:** Chapters 38–40, the Goose-Wife everyone feared turns out kind: everyone said she turned travelers into geese, and she didn't. Chapter 43, Alfie reads the story from the pictures, Hugh checks it, and Alfie was right.

### 16. Make Something New

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kid name** | Make Something New |
| **Grown-up name** | Creativity and imagination |
| **Kid line** | I dream it up and make it real |
| **Family · season** | Wise (Prudence) · Season 3, Parts 10–12 (the finale) |
| **Status** | Proposed, except the names and kid line |

**Golden mean**

| | Too little | **Make Something New** | Too much |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kid words** | **Only copying:** "I can't draw." "Tell me what to make." Always the coloring page, never your own idea. | Dream up your own idea, and make it real: try it, fix it, finish it. | **Never finishing:** ten ideas and none made, starting over every time it's hard, new just to be different even when the old way worked. |
| **Grown-up words** | Imitation with no invention; fear of trying. | Creativity: new *and* made real, with craft. | Novelty without craft; scattered starts; change for its own sake. |

**Tag:** `Make Something New · between only copying and never finishing`
**Sides:** too_little = only copying · too_much = never finishing
*The pair is ours. Copying is how everyone starts: Aristotle says people are "the most imitative of living creatures, and through imitation" learn their "earliest lessons" (Poetics 4, S. H. Butcher's translation). So the failing is* only *copying. The never-finishing side is where creativity meets Keep Going.*

**Phrase and gesture**
- **Phrase:** "Dream it… make it!"
- **Gesture:** Draw a big circle in the air with one finger (dream it). Then pat and squeeze it into shape with both hands, like clay (make it).
- **Little gesture:** draw a big circle in the air.

**Tuesday pair** (example): an apprentice scribe who will only trace other people's letters and won't write a word of his own (only copying) / a clever girl with a hundred half-built inventions in her shed and not one that works (never finishing). The scribe writes his own name, big; the girl picks one invention and finishes it with the children's help.

**Three modeled behaviors**
1. Use something ordinary in a new way: a box becomes a boat, a spoon becomes a drumstick. (Part 10)
2. When your first try goes wrong, change one thing and try again, or turn the mistake into something new. (Part 11)
3. Finish one small thing, show it, and say how you made it. (Part 12)

**"I can…" ladder and if-then plans**

| Rung | "I can…" | What a parent sees | If-then plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Little** (about 3–4) | I can make something from my own idea. | She draws her own picture instead of a coloring page. A box becomes a car. She makes up a song. She builds something and gives it a name. | If you put out paper and crayons, then I draw my own picture. |
| **Middle** (about 5–6) | I can turn a mistake into something new, and finish what I start. | A scribble becomes a picture. He invents a game with its own rules and teaches it. He builds a boat from a carton and tests it in the bath. He finishes one drawing before starting the next. | If it goes wrong, then I change one thing and keep making. |
| **Big** (about 7–9) | I can plan, make, test and fix my own creation. | They design a paper plane, test it, and change one fold at a time. They write and illustrate a short story of a few pages. They make a gift from things at home. They explain their idea and the steps they took. | If my first version doesn't work, then I test it, fix one thing, and try again before starting something else. |
| **Stretch** | I can make something new that helps someone, make it with others, and finish a project that takes days. | She builds a bird feeder, or invents a fix for something annoying at home. She writes a short play for her siblings and puts it on. She finishes a project over several days. She asks for ideas and uses them to make it better. | If I want to quit halfway, then I pick the next small step and do just that one. |

**Moved up a rung** (see rule 8)
- *Middle, for a child of 3–4:* They turn a scribble into a sun or a cat, and tell you what it is. They make up a new verse for a song they know.
- *Big, for a child of 5–6:* He plans a paper crown, makes it, tries it on, and fixes the part that slips. He draws a three-page story with a beginning, a middle and an end.

**If-then cards**
- **When…** I have an idea · it goes wrong · I want to quit halfway
- **Then I…** say "Dream it… make it!" and start · change one thing and try again · do the next small step and finish

**Classical echoes**
- **Folk tales and stories:** *The Magic Paintbrush*, a Chinese folk tale: Ma Liang's paintings come true, and he paints only to help people. Kipling, *How the Alphabet Was Made* in *Just So Stories*: Taffy and her father turn pictures of sounds into the first letters. It suits a season in a library, and it echoes Hugh's hard old letters (Chapter 42).
