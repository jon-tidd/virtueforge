3 · THE READING PAGE · BUILT ON HOW KIDS LEARN

Every chapter follows the same sequence: remember, choose, struggle, pause, and see goodness rewarded.

It opens by recalling last night. The hero stops at a real choice and you pause to ask a question. The hero struggles before doing the right thing. Warm nightlight colors, and the phone faces you, not your child.

Why: asking questions while reading builds language and comprehension (dialogic reading, Whitehurst). A character who struggles and then succeeds inspires more than a perfect one (Schunk's "coping models").

4 · THE LAST PAGE · WHERE HABITS START

Name who they were tonight. Give them one small mission for tomorrow. End on a cliffhanger.

The final page gives your child an identity ("a brave-hearted one"), then a mission they can actually do tomorrow. The next chapter unlocks tomorrow night, so the reward for showing up is more story, not points.

Why: calling a child "a helper" makes them help more than asking them "to help" (Bryan, Master & Walton 2014). A specific "when X, I'll do Y" plan works better than a general goal (Gollwitzer). Prizes for being good can backfire, so reward reading, never behavior (Warneken & Tomasello).

5 · THE NEXT DAY · THE STORY REMEMBERS

You notice something good in real life. That night, the story tells it back as a legend.

This is the loop no other bedtime app has: story, then mission, then real life, then a parent who notices, then the story again. Kids are the audience for their own good deeds. It also changes you: you spend the day watching for good behavior instead of correcting.

Why: character is "caught, taught, and sought" (Jubilee Centre). Praising what a child actually did works better than praising in general. And stories where honesty is rewarded made kids more honest, while stories that punish lying (Pinocchio, The Boy Who Cried Wolf) didn't (Lee et al. 2014).
