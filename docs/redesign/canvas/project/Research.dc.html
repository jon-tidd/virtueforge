The science, plainly

Every design choice has a reason. Here they are.

We read the research on stories, character and habits, and built only what the evidence supports. Each finding below shows what we do because of it.

Children who see themselves in a story act on it more

In a study of 315 five- and six-year-olds, those who heard a sharing story with their own picture and name shared more afterward. Just swapping in a name did nothing.

Early Childhood Education Journal, 2025; Kruse et al., 2021
So your kids are drawn into every chapter, consistently, not just named.

Human heroes teach better than talking animals

Preschoolers shared more after a story with human characters. The same story told with animals made them share less.

Larsen, Lee & Ganea, Developmental Science, 2018
So the heroes are your children. Animals are sidekicks.

Reward the virtue; don't just punish the vice

Hearing that honesty led to something good made children more truthful. Pinocchio and The Boy Who Cried Wolf didn't.

Lee et al., Psychological Science, 2014; Liang et al., 2025
So every chapter shows what goes right when someone does the right thing.

Explaining beats being told

Five- and six-year-olds who were asked to explain a story carried its lesson to new situations better than those told the moral.

Walker & Lombrozo, Cognition, 2017
So each chapter has a "Why do you think…?" pause, never a lecture.

Talking during reading builds language

Asking open questions while reading has moderate, reliable effects on children's vocabulary.

Dialogic reading meta-analyses, 2008–2025
So there's one pause-and-ask moment built into every chapter.

Stories that continue keep children engaged

Children stayed engaged with a serialized story over time; engagement with stand-alone episodes faded.

Lu, Green et al., Communication Research, 2025
So it's one adventure per season, and the kids choose where it goes next.

Prizes for kindness can backfire

Toddlers rewarded with a toy for helping later helped less. Those who were thanked didn't.

Warneken & Tomasello, 2008
So we celebrate reading together and tell good deeds back as stories. No points for being good.

A routine most nights is what counts

Bedtime routine benefits grow with each night, with clear gains at five or more nights a week. Screens before bed delay sleep.

Mindell et al., Sleep, 2015; American Academy of Pediatrics
So five nights a week is a great week, and every chapter can be printed or played.

A familiar voice matters

Children's brains respond far more strongly to their mother's voice than to a stranger's.

Abrams et al., PNAS, 2016
So you, or Grandma, can record the chapters in your own voice.
What we don't claim
No app can promise a more virtuous child. Studies like these measure short-term effects in controlled settings. We promise a method built on the best evidence we could find, and we'll update it when the evidence changes.
